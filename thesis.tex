\begin{abstractpage}
The transition toward low-carbon energy systems requires reliable monitoring of underwater infrastructure, including subsea pipelines, carbon dioxide sequestration sites, and environmentally sensitive marine environments. Conventional underwater vehicles are often limited by high cost, limited maneuverability in confined regions, and dependence on bulky sensing and localization systems. This dissertation presents the design, modeling, and control of an intelligent bio-inspired robotic fish platform for energy-transition applications, with emphasis on pipeline tracking, environmental sensing, depth regulation, and underwater relative localization.

The robotic fish is developed as a compact, low-cost, and maneuverable underwater platform integrating bio-inspired propulsion, embedded sensing, vision-based perception, depth-control hardware, and wireless sensing modules. A system-level design framework is presented, including mechanical design, electronics integration, actuator selection, buoyancy management, and real-time embedded control. Dynamic models are developed for key subsystems, including the robotic fish motion and a variable-buoyancy/depth-control device. Model-based and feedback control strategies are implemented to regulate swimming behavior and depth while accounting for actuator constraints and practical experimental limitations.

For infrastructure monitoring, the dissertation develops vision-based pipeline tracking methods that allow the robotic fish to detect and follow underwater pipeline-like features using onboard camera feedback. Environmental monitoring capabilities are investigated through oil-spill sensing experiments and carbon dioxide leak-source localization concepts relevant to subsea sequestration monitoring. In addition, an underwater relative-localization framework is developed using received signal strength indicator measurements from multiple antenna channels, fused with inertial and magnetic sensing to estimate relative distance and bearing between underwater nodes or robotic platforms.

The proposed methods are validated through hardware development, benchtop testing, pool experiments, and data-driven analysis. The results demonstrate that an intelligent robotic fish can serve as an integrated experimental platform for underwater mobility, sensing, and autonomy in energy-transition applications. By combining bio-inspired locomotion, depth control, visual servoing, environmental sensing, and RSS-based localization, this work contributes toward scalable autonomous underwater systems for safer monitoring of subsea infrastructure and emerging carbon-management technologies.
\end{abstractpage}

\chapter{Introduction}
Oceans cover approximately 70\% of the Earth's surface and form the planet's largest habitable environment. They regulate climate, support marine ecosystems and food resources, provide major routes for transportation and global communication, and contain resources relevant to energy production and scientific discovery \cite{NationalGeographic2024,NOAA2026}. Their importance is therefore environmental, economic, and infrastructural, extending well beyond the coastal regions that are most familiar and accessible to human activity.

Despite this importance, knowledge of the ocean remains incomplete and direct exploration remains extremely limited. Satellite-derived data provide a general map of the global seafloor, but only 28.7\% had been mapped using modern high-resolution technology as of April 2026. Direct visual exploration is even more limited: less than 0.001\% of the deep-ocean seafloor has been observed by explorers \cite{NOAA2026}. These measures describe different aspects of exploration and should not be combined into a single universal percentage, but they demonstrate the scale of what remains unknown. Human use of the ocean is similarly concentrated in comparatively accessible surface, coastal, and offshore regions, while much of the deep ocean remains outside routine scientific observation or sustained utilization.

The central barrier is access. Underwater operations must contend with limited visibility, increasing hydrostatic pressure, uncertain currents, low temperatures, corrosion, and severe communication and localization constraints. Reaching and observing underwater regions can require research vessels, specialized crews, pressure-tolerant equipment, and substantial deployment and recovery effort \cite{Shukla2016,Mcleod2012,Patel2024}. Underwater robotic systems extend the reach of human observation into these environments and provide a means to explore, measure, and interact with regions that cannot be accessed routinely by human operators.

This dissertation addresses this need through the development and use of a compact bio-inspired robotic fish platform. Robotic-fish locomotion, buoyancy control, perception, chemical sensing, and localization are developed as connected capabilities required for a small underwater robot to inspect infrastructure, collect environmental measurements, regulate its position in three-dimensional water environments, and move toward cooperative monitoring. The introduction establishes the motivation and prior work across the four main technical directions of the dissertation: design and modeling of the underwater robot and buoyancy device, model validation and control design, safety inspection and monitoring of underwater infrastructure, and cooperative inspection through relative localization. Detailed hardware drawings, equations, controller synthesis, sensing algorithms, and experimental results are reserved for the corresponding technical chapters.

\section{Underwater Infrastructure Monitoring}

Offshore oil and gas development is a prominent example of ocean utilization. It relies on an extensive network of subsea pipelines to transport petroleum products between offshore facilities and onshore processing infrastructure. Unlike land-based pipelines, these systems operate in a difficult-to-access environment and are exposed to hydrostatic pressure, low temperature, corrosive seawater, external loading, seabed movement, pressure cycling, material defects, and accidental impact. Regular inspection is therefore required to identify corrosion, mechanical damage, leakage, biofouling, and structural degradation before they lead to operational failure or environmental harm \cite{Ho2020,Shukla2016,Mcleod2012,Patel2024}.

The monitoring problem is also expanding beyond conventional petroleum infrastructure. Carbon capture and storage (CCS) creates additional subsea monitoring needs because captured \mbox{CO$_2$} must be transported and stored without uncontrolled release \cite{Herzog2001,Yadav2022,Osman2021}. Petroleum-system failures can release crude oil or gas, while carbon-management failures can release dense-phase or gaseous \mbox{CO$_2$} into seawater. The consequences can include ecological toxicity, seawater acidification, and long-term disturbance to marine habitats \cite{Filonchyk2024,Kabir2023,Zheng2021,Mortezaei2021,Queiros2014,Pham2016}. Although the release mechanisms differ, both cases require early detection, spatial localization, environmental measurement, and follow-up inspection near the suspected source.

Pipeline leak detection is commonly divided into internal and external approaches. Internal approaches infer leakage from measurements inside the pipeline, including mass-volume balance, acoustic or pressure-wave methods, pressure point analysis, inverse transient analysis, and simplified pressure-analysis techniques \cite{Martins2010,Hou2013,Akib2011,Vitkovsky2007,Ostapkowicz2016}. These methods can support continuous surveillance and can often identify that an abnormal condition has occurred. External inspection and environmental sensing are still needed to map the surrounding region, measure contaminant spread, and inspect the external condition of the pipeline after a suspected event. Internal methods are also sensitive to instrumentation quality, flow conditions, and measurement noise, which can increase false alarms or uncertainty in leak localization \cite{Reda2024}.

External approaches inspect the pipeline or the surrounding water directly. These include visual inspection, acoustic monitoring, fiber-optic sensing, fluorometric detection, capacitive sensing, chemical sensing, and robotic surveys \cite{Ho2020,Jasper2012,Lukonge2020,Adegboye2019}. External approaches can provide more direct physical evidence of leakage or contamination, but their value depends on the platform that carries the sensor. Fixed sensors are useful only near their installation point. Surface and aerial systems can observe large-scale surface slicks but are less suitable for early detection near a submerged source. ROV- or AUV-mounted sensors can reach the subsea region, but deployment cost and operational complexity limit how frequently they can be used for small-scale or distributed monitoring.

CCS monitoring adds distinct engineering and environmental constraints. \mbox{CO$_2$} pipelines may experience fracture and decompression behavior influenced by pressure, phase transition, and impurities in the transported stream \cite{Aursand2016,Cosham2009}. If \mbox{CO$_2$} escapes into seawater, it can dissolve and form carbonic acid, locally changing pH and potentially affecting benthic organisms, fish larvae, and other marine communities \cite{Zheng2021,Mortezaei2021,Queiros2014,Pham2016}. Monitoring therefore includes both mechanical pipe condition and environmental measurements that indicate where the leaked substance has moved after release.

Prior work has investigated mobile robotic platforms for related inspection problems. Zhang et al.~\cite{Zhang2021} studied AUV-based subsea pipeline leak inspection. Qasem et al.~\cite{Qasem2019} developed a multisensor ROV for oil-spill surveillance. Hu et al.~\cite{Hu2022} investigated robotic-fish-based underwater gas leak detection using onboard camera data. These studies show that mobile underwater sensing can add spatial information beyond fixed instrumentation. They also motivate the central systems question of this dissertation: how can a compact bio-inspired robotic fish be designed, controlled, and instrumented so that inspection, environmental sensing, and relative localization can be studied on a common platform?

\section{Limitations of Conventional Platforms}

ROVs and AUVs remain the dominant platforms for subsea inspection. ROVs can carry large payloads and provide real-time operator feedback, but they are tethered systems that require surface vessels, skilled operators, and substantial deployment cost \cite{Capocci2017,Shukla2016}. AUVs reduce tether dependence and can execute preplanned missions, but many are still large, expensive vehicles designed for broad survey tasks rather than compact inspection near confined pipeline corridors or environmentally sensitive regions \cite{Mcleod2012,Patel2024}. In high-value industrial inspection, these costs can be justified because the infrastructure being inspected is expensive and failure can be catastrophic. For repeated environmental monitoring, laboratory-scale method development, distributed coverage, or operation in smaller water bodies, the same cost and logistical requirements become limiting.

The propulsion and sensing footprint of conventional vehicles is another limitation. Many inspection-class vehicles rely on propellers or thrusters to generate maneuvering forces. These devices are effective and controllable, but they can introduce local turbulence, disturb sediment, alter the local sensing environment, and affect marine life in ways that are undesirable for ecological monitoring \cite{Marras2012,Polverino2013}. For chemical sensing, this disturbance can also change the local concentration field by mixing the water near the sensor. For visual inspection, sediment disturbance can reduce visibility and degrade image-based feature extraction. These issues motivate low-speed, low-disturbance vehicle designs for local monitoring tasks with modest transit-speed and payload requirements.

Navigation and localization impose additional constraints. Because GPS signals attenuate rapidly in seawater, underwater vehicles commonly rely on acoustic long-baseline (LBL), short-baseline (SBL), or ultra-short-baseline (USBL) systems, sometimes combined with Doppler velocity logs (DVLs) and inertial sensors \cite{Luo2021,Su2020,Zekavat2019}. These systems can provide high accuracy, but the supporting acoustic hardware, deployment infrastructure, power consumption, and cost are difficult to scale down to small robotic fish or multi-robot groups. Recent acoustic work has sought more compact solutions, including passive inverted ultra-short baseline (piUSBL), passive iUSBL for small AUVs, and MEMS-microphone-based acoustic localization and communication \cite{Rypkema2019,Wang2022,Hinduja2022}. These efforts motivate complementary low-cost relative-localization methods for compact cooperative monitoring platforms.

The same issue appears in sensing. High-end sonars, Doppler instruments, acoustic positioning systems, and large chemical payloads can be appropriate for full-scale AUVs, while compact robotic fish require sensors that are lightweight, low-power, waterproof, and compatible with limited onboard processing and communication. The design problem is therefore a system problem involving the platform, power system, actuator configuration, sensor interfaces, waterproofing, communication pathway, and software. This system-level requirement is one of the motivations for developing AquaNavigator as a common experimental platform across the monitoring tasks.

\section{Bio-Inspired Robotic Fish}
Bio-inspired underwater robots offer an alternative class of underwater platform for medium speed with low-disturbance monitoring. Fish-like propulsion is grounded in classical models of aquatic animal locomotion, including Lighthill's elongated-body and large-amplitude elongated-body theories \cite{Lighthill1969,Lighthill1971}. Robotic-fish research has since expanded into design, fabrication, hydrodynamics, control, and sensing, with survey work summarizing the state of biomimetic fish robots and body and/or caudal fin swimming mechanisms \cite{Yu2017,Duraisamy2019,Xie2021}. Because robotic fish can be compact and can move with oscillatory body or tail motion, they received attention from researchers as mobile sensor platforms for underwater environments \cite{Tan2011}. Their wake and motion can also be less disruptive to ocean echosystem than mainstream propeller-based ROV platforms in most conditions \cite{Marras2012,Polverino2013}.

Early robotic-fish research emerged during the 1990s from efforts to reproduce biological swimming using mechanically and materially inspired actuation. Shahinpoor proposed swimming robotic structures actuated by ionic polymeric gel muscles in 1992 \cite{Shahinpoor1992}. MIT's RoboTuna, first demonstrated in 1994, subsequently provided a platform for experimentally studying fish-like body and caudal-fin propulsion and the hydrodynamics of articulated swimming \cite{Thomson1994,Tolkoff1999}. Research during the latter half of the decade broadened the design space to include pectoral-fin mechanisms and flexible or oscillating foils for underwater propulsion and maneuvering \cite{Imaizumi1995,Kato1996}. These early systems established the feasibility of mechanically reproducing fish-like propulsion while exposing practical challenges involving joint coordination, traveling-wave generation, actuator packaging, and underwater implementation.

During the 2000s, robotic-fish research made progress from propulsion feasibility toward actively controlled bio-inspired platforms. Mason and Burdick investigated carangiform locomotion using a three-link experimental robot, while Yu et al. developed multijoint robotic fish with controlled swimming speed and orientation \cite{Mason2000,Yu2004,Yu2005}. Subsequent systems demonstrated compact three-dimensional swimming, modular undulating fins, and alternative soft or smart-material actuators \cite{Liu2005,Low2006,Chen2010}. From the 2010s onward, the research focus expanded beyond propulsion to include robotic fish as mobile sensor platforms, vision-based target following, visual-inertial localization, soft robotic exploration near marine ecosystem, energy-aware propulsion, and increasingly sophisticated motion planning \cite{Tan2011,Hu2009,Wang2015,Katzschmann2018,Li2024,Jiang2024}. Other work also developed dynamic models connecting caudal-fin kinematics with thrust, drag, and body motion \cite{Wang2013,Park2012}. This progression reflects a broader transition from reproducing fish-like motion toward using robotic fish as integrated platforms for perception, sensing, control, and underwater monitoring.

Across these developments, biological swimming modes provide a common framework for selecting a robotic propulsion architecture. Fish locomotion is broadly classified into body and/or caudal fin (BCF) propulsion and median and/or paired fin (MPF) propulsion, with individual modes employing oscillatory or undulatory motion \cite{Sfakiotakis1999}. BCF locomotion includes anguilliform, subcarangiform, carangiform, thunniform, and ostraciiform patterns, distinguished primarily by how lateral motion is distributed along the body. This classification is directly relevant to AquaNavigator. Its carangiform-inspired arrangement concentrates motion toward the posterior body and caudal fin, making a wave-like propulsion to be approximated using a compact, mechanically coupled tail without independently actuating each tail joint \cite{Xie2021,Zuo2021}. The detailed double-slider-crank mechanism and its kinematic implications are presented in the platform-design chapter.

However, the literature also shows a gap between robotic-fish locomotion research and complete monitoring systems. Many robotic fish are developed to demonstrate swimming performance, maneuverability, locomotion efficiency, or tracking of visual targets. Fewer studies integrate the full chain required for underwater monitoring: a physical platform, three-dimensional positioning capability, onboard perception, environmental sensing payloads, and a path toward cooperative operation. For subsea infrastructure monitoring, the robot must move in a controlled way near a feature, carry sensors while maintaining stability, regulate its vertical position, collect meaningful measurements, and communicate or localize relative to other agents.

The AquaNavigator platform used in this dissertation is developed within this broader need. It uses bio-inspired tail-driven propulsion as the mobility foundation and serves as the common experimental base through which buoyancy control, pipeline tracking, \mbox{CO$_2$} sensing, oil-spill detection, and relative localization are connected \cite{Masood2025Pipeline,Masood2025CO2,Masood2026OilSpill}. This framing is important for the dissertation: the thesis contribution combines platform development, modeling, control, sensing, and localization into a coherent robotic fish system for underwater monitoring research.

\section{3D Motion and Buoyancy Control}

Many robotic-fish studies focus primarily on planar swimming, where speed and heading are the dominant controlled variables. Infrastructure inspection and environmental sensing often require more than planar motion: the robot must reach a depth, hold a vertical position, profile through the water column, or position a sensor near a leak source. Variable buoyancy is a natural approach for compact, low-speed underwater robots because it changes the net buoyant force by changing displaced volume or effective density, rather than requiring continuous vertical thrust. This matters for monitoring because a chemical sensor, camera, or localization device is useful when the robot can position it in the relevant part of the water column. For example, a pipeline-following mission may require the robot to remain near the pipeline height, while dissolved \mbox{CO$_2$} sensing may require spatial sampling through a concentration field. Depth regulation therefore expands the monitoring capability from planar motion toward three-dimensional operation.

Variable buoyancy mechanisms have been developed using seawater transfer, gas generation, compressed air, electroactive polymers, electrochemical systems, and electrothermal expansion \cite{Kobayashi2010,Vasiljevic2019,Um2011,Chen2017,Yazji2021,Wood2015,Hou2020}. Depth-control work has also been reported for buoyancy-driven AUVs, underwater units, gliders, and buoyancy change devices \cite{Choyekh2016,Claus2012,Carneiro2023}. These mechanisms show that buoyancy control can be implemented in many ways, but the design tradeoffs are different for a compact robotic fish than for a large glider or profiling float. A robotic fish has limited internal volume, limited power, tight waterproofing constraints, and a body geometry that is already shaped by hydrodynamic and payload requirements. The buoyancy device must therefore be compact, waterproof, mechanically simple, responsive enough for short-range depth regulation, and compatible with the robot's payload and electronics. The piston-cylinder buoyancy device considered in this dissertation introduces hard actuator stroke and voltage limits, pressure-dependent behavior, and nonlinearities that must be considered in the control design. This motivates model-based and constraint-aware approaches, including constrained model predictive control and extremum seeking control \cite{Rawlings2017,Scokaert1998,Masood2023,Masood2026ESCBCD}.

The control challenge includes nonlinear buoyancy dynamics and tight actuation constraints. The device operates around a buoyancy equilibrium that can be sensitive to small changes in volume, added mass, trapped air, leakage, and sensor noise. The actuator has finite stroke, finite speed, and voltage limits. If these limits are ignored, a controller may request physically impossible commands or push the system into undesirable operating regions. Constrained model predictive control is therefore relevant because it can represent actuator limits explicitly in the control problem \cite{Rawlings2017,Scokaert1998}. At the same time, model uncertainty motivates a complementary controller that can work with less dependence on an exact plant model. This is why the dissertation treats model validation and control design as a chapter-level topic rather than presenting the buoyancy device only as a mechanical component.

\section{Perception and Environmental Sensing}

The monitoring value of a robotic fish depends on its ability to collect useful information while swimming. This dissertation treats perception and environmental sensing as one connected thesis theme: the same underwater platform is used for vision-based pipeline tracking, \mbox{CO$_2$} leak-source localization, and oil-spill detection. These tasks differ in sensing modality, but they are linked by the requirement that the robot move through an underwater environment while producing interpretable measurements about infrastructure or environmental state. The technical chapters separate the control aspects from the environmental-sensing payloads, while keeping the system role consistent: AquaNavigator acts as a mobile carrier that positions a sensor, collects data, and links the measurement to the robot's motion. The following subsections introduce the motivation for each sensing direction.

\subsection{Vision-Based Pipeline Tracking}

Pipeline inspection requires a robot to detect a pipeline-like feature and regulate its motion relative to that feature. Vision-based inspection has been studied for ROVs and underwater vehicles, including image-based visual servoing approaches for underwater pipeline inspection \cite{Yi2023}. Image-based visual servoing (IBVS) is attractive because control errors are computed directly from image-space measurements, reducing dependence on full three-dimensional reconstruction or global localization \cite{Reynolds1999}. For a small robotic fish, this is particularly useful because accurate global underwater localization is difficult, while a camera can provide immediate information about the pipeline's apparent orientation and location in the image.

The pipeline-tracking task also exposes an important platform-level challenge. The robotic fish is underactuated and moves through oscillatory tail propulsion rather than independent surge, sway, and yaw thrusters. The image-processing output must therefore be converted into steering and propulsion commands that are compatible with the vehicle dynamics. The pipeline may also appear with changing contrast, lighting, partial occlusion, and perspective effects. The sensing and control problem therefore closes the loop between image-space error and a bio-inspired underwater vehicle.

In this dissertation, the pipeline-tracking work develops an IBVS framework for the underactuated AquaNavigator robotic fish using onboard camera feedback and Hough-transform pipeline detection \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}. At the introduction level, this work demonstrates the perception layer of the thesis: the robotic fish can use onboard visual information to regulate motion relative to an infrastructure feature. The detailed image-processing pipeline, error variables, controller implementation, and pool-validation results are presented in the model-validation and control chapter.

\subsection[CO2 Leak-Source Localization]{\mbox{CO$_2$} Leak-Source Localization}

CCS monitoring requires sensing methods that can identify whether stored or transported \mbox{CO$_2$} remains contained. The motivation begins with the broader climate context: increasing atmospheric \mbox{CO$_2$} concentration is a major driver of climate change, and CCS is one proposed strategy for reducing emissions from industrial sources \cite{Filonchyk2024,Kabir2023,Herzog2001,Yadav2022,Osman2021}. After capture, the monitoring problem continues through transport, injection, and storage, where releases into the surrounding environment must be detected.

Prior work has discussed the environmental risk of \mbox{CO$_2$} leakage, ocean-storage consequences, and the need for monitoring near subsea pipelines and storage sites \cite{Zheng2021,Mortezaei2021,Queiros2014,Pham2016}. Mobile robots can support this task by collecting spatially distributed measurements instead of relying on fixed sensors alone. A release may dissolve, rise, diffuse, advect with water motion, or form a spatial concentration pattern that must be sampled over an area. Thus, a mobile sensing platform can provide information about both the presence of \mbox{CO$_2$} and the spatial structure of the concentration field.

Prior robotic inspection work has addressed gas leaks using visual bubble detection, sonar-based inspection, or multisensor ROV systems \cite{Zhang2021,Qasem2019,Hu2022}. These methods are useful, but \mbox{CO$_2$} source localization also benefits from chemical concentration measurements when visible bubbles are weak, intermittent, or absent. The robotic fish provides a way to investigate this sensing problem in a compact platform whose motion and payload can be controlled in a laboratory water environment.

This dissertation uses the robotic fish as a carrier for near-infrared dissolved \mbox{CO$_2$} sensing and leak-source localization in controlled water experiments \cite{Masood2025CO2}. The chapter-level treatment focuses on the sensing setup, spatial data processing, source estimation, and the limitations of controlled-environment validation. The introduction frames the work as one part of the broader thesis argument: a robotic fish can serve as a mobile environmental sensing platform for energy-transition infrastructure.

\subsection{Oil-Spill Detection}

Oil-spill monitoring includes both pipeline-oriented leak detection and environmental contamination assessment. Offshore drilling and subsea transport remain major parts of the global energy system \cite{HeinrichBoellStiftung2017}. Crude oil is valuable as an energy resource, but uncontrolled release into aquatic environments can harm marine organisms, seabirds, coastal ecosystems, and human activities that depend on marine resources \cite{Almeda2014,Albers1982}. Oil-spill response therefore depends on early detection, localization of the release region, and monitoring of how contamination changes with time.

Internal pipeline methods can indicate that a leak has occurred, while external methods provide more direct information near the leak or contaminated region. Internal methods include mass-balance monitoring, pressure-wave analysis, pressure point analysis, inverse transient methods, and related model-based or signal-processing techniques \cite{Martins2010,Hou2013,Akib2011,Vitkovsky2007,Ostapkowicz2016,Reda2024}. These methods are valuable because they can run continuously and can be integrated into pipeline operations. Because they generally infer leakage indirectly from internal measurements, external sensing is needed to describe where oil has moved after release into the surrounding water.

External methods inspect the surrounding environment using direct sensing or imaging. Acoustic, optical, fluorometric, fiber-optic, capacitive, and chemical sensing approaches have all been investigated for pipeline leak detection and oil-spill monitoring \cite{Jasper2012,Lukonge2020,Adegboye2019,Yao2024,Du2025}. ROV-based oil-spill recognition systems and multisensor underwater vehicles show the value of deploying sensors close to the leak region \cite{Qasem2019,Pocwiardowski2021}. Many of these systems remain tied to relatively large platforms, expensive deployments, or sensors whose size and support requirements are difficult to integrate into a compact mobile robot.

Organic electrochemical transistor (OECT) sensors have recently been demonstrated for crude oil detection in subsea environments \cite{Porter2024}. This sensing direction is important because it moves beyond using a camera alone to infer contamination visually. An OECT can respond to contact with oil through changes in the electrical behavior of the device, allowing chemical detection to be considered as a robotic payload. Robotic use then requires packaging, waterproofing, power, compact readout circuitry, sensor placement that can contact the contaminant, and interpretation during robot motion.

This dissertation integrates an OECT sensing module with AquaNavigator so that chemical oil detection can be evaluated on a moving robotic fish during pipeline-following experiments \cite{Masood2026OilSpill}. The contribution combines sensing, packaging, signal conditioning, robot motion, and real-time detection in a controlled pool environment. This work is therefore presented with the \mbox{CO$_2$} monitoring study in the environmental-sensing chapter, while the pipeline-tracking controller that supports the oil-spill experiment is developed in the preceding control chapter.

\section{Cooperative Inspection and Relative Localization}

A single robotic fish can inspect only a limited spatial region during a mission. For larger underwater assets or distributed environmental monitoring, cooperative groups of small robots can potentially increase coverage, reduce inspection time, and provide redundancy. Underwater swarm robotics remains difficult because communication, localization, and sensing are strongly constrained by the medium \cite{Connor2021}. Acoustic communication and localization are widely used, but size, power, bandwidth, and multi-user interference become important barriers for small robotic fish. Optical methods can provide high data rates over short distances, but they are sensitive to turbidity, scattering, and alignment \cite{Partan2007}.

Localization is central to cooperation. In terrestrial robotics, robots can often combine GPS, wheel odometry, cameras, lidar, or radio-based positioning. Underwater localization uses different sensing modalities because GPS signals attenuate after submergence, and visual localization is strongly affected by turbidity, illumination, suspended particles, and feature availability. Acoustic localization methods are mature and widely used, but they usually require specialized transducers, timing, synchronization, and deployment geometry \cite{Luo2021,Su2020}. For small robotic fish, the size, cost, and power requirements of these systems are a major constraint.

Radio-frequency (RF) methods are usually limited underwater, especially at high frequencies, but lower-frequency RF has been investigated for short-range underwater communication and localization \cite{Palmeiro2011,Nguyen2019,Ryecroft2019}. The RSS-based localization literature also provides a basis for estimating range or position from received signal strength when the propagation model and hardware characteristics are understood \cite{Zekavat2019,Nguyen2019}. In this dissertation, RF is investigated as a complementary short-range sensing modality for compact robots, especially in controlled or freshwater environments where low-frequency RF communication may remain measurable over useful distances. The difficulty is that underwater RF propagation is strongly affected by frequency, conductivity, antenna geometry, enclosure effects, multipath, and robot orientation. These effects make a simple path-loss inversion insufficient for reliable relative localization.

In this dissertation, the cooperative-inspection direction is developed through relative localization. The work develops and evaluates sub-GHz RSSI-based relative localization with directional antennas, inertial/magnetic sensing, and data-driven compensation for practical hardware and water-tank effects \cite{Masood2026RSSI,Masood2025Swarm}. This provides a step toward cooperative robotic-fish monitoring through the demonstrated relative-localization framework. The connection to the rest of the thesis is system-level: after a single robotic fish can move, regulate depth, and collect sensor data, relative localization becomes one of the requirements for future multi-robot inspection. The localization chapter therefore extends the dissertation from a single-platform monitoring system toward cooperative inspection.

\section{Research Objectives}

The dissertation is organized around four connected objectives. First, it develops and models the AquaNavigator robotic fish and its buoyancy device as the physical platform for the thesis. Second, it validates the buoyancy-device model and develops control methods for depth regulation under practical actuator constraints. Third, it uses the robotic fish for safety inspection and environmental monitoring through pipeline tracking, \mbox{CO$_2$} leak-source localization, and oil-spill detection. Fourth, it develops a relative-localization framework that supports the longer-term goal of cooperative inspection by compact underwater robots.

These objectives lead to the following specific research tasks:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Design and model a bio-inspired underwater robotic fish platform with a double-slider-crank tail mechanism, steering capability, embedded electronics, sensor interfaces, and a piston-cylinder variable buoyancy device.
\item Develop and validate dynamic models for the robotic fish and buoyancy device, and design depth-control methods that account for actuator saturation, unstable buoyancy dynamics, and practical implementation constraints \cite{Masood2023,Masood2026ESCBCD}.
\item Develop a vision-based pipeline tracking framework and evaluate it as a safety inspection capability for underwater infrastructure \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}.
\item Integrate and evaluate environmental sensing payloads for \mbox{CO$_2$} leak-source localization and OECT-based oil-spill detection in controlled water experiments \cite{Masood2025CO2,Masood2026OilSpill}.
\item Develop and experimentally evaluate an underwater relative-localization framework using sub-GHz RSSI, directional antennas, and inertial/magnetic sensing for cooperative inspection scenarios \cite{Masood2026RSSI,Masood2025Swarm}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

The main contributions of this dissertation are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Robotic fish and buoyancy-device platform:} A compact bio-inspired underwater robotic fish platform is developed as a reusable experimental base for three-dimensional motion, sensing, and inspection-oriented research.
\item \textbf{Model validation and control design:} Dynamic modeling and validation of the buoyancy device are connected to constrained MPC and constraint-aware ESC approaches for depth regulation \cite{Masood2023,Masood2026ESCBCD}.
\item \textbf{Infrastructure perception:} A camera-based pipeline tracking framework is developed for the underactuated robotic fish and evaluated in water tank experiments \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}.
\item \textbf{Environmental monitoring payload integration:} The robotic fish is used as a carrier for \mbox{CO$_2$} leak-source localization and OECT-based oil-spill detection, demonstrating how multiple sensing modalities can be integrated on the same underwater platform \cite{Masood2025CO2,Masood2026OilSpill}.
\item \textbf{Relative localization toward cooperative inspection:} A compact sub-GHz RSSI and inertial/magnetic sensing framework is developed for relative localization in leader-follower underwater monitoring scenarios \cite{Masood2026RSSI,Masood2025Swarm}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Design of the Buoyancy Control Device}

The BCD is designed to change the displaced volume of an underwater body while keeping its mass approximately constant. Changing the displaced volume changes the net buoyant force, which provides a direct route for vertical motion and depth regulation without continuous vertical thruster effort. The design uses a piston-cylinder assembly driven by a miniature linear actuator. When the piston moves, the effective volume of the device changes and the device becomes positively or negatively buoyant depending on the piston direction.

The design requirements are set by the robotic-fish application. The device must be compact enough to integrate with a small underwater robot, mechanically simple enough to be sealed, and repeatable enough to support controller design. It must also respect hard physical limits. The linear actuator has finite stroke, finite speed, finite voltage, friction, backlash, and direction-dependent behavior. The piston chamber is sealed, so internal pressure changes as the piston moves. The external hydrostatic pressure also changes with depth. These features make the BCD a coupled buoyancy-actuator system rather than an ideal volume source.

\section{Design of AquaNavigator}

AquaNavigator is designed as a bio-inspired robotic fish for underwater inspection and environmental monitoring experiments. The design methodology follows three parts: the caudal-fin propulsion system, the electronics and buoyancy enclosure, and the passive body covers that give the platform its fish-like shape. The design begins from tail-based propulsion because the robot's motion is generated by an oscillatory caudal-fin mechanism rather than by conventional propellers.

\subsection{Tail Propulsion and Steering}

The propulsion design is inspired by undulatory fish locomotion. The lateral displacement of a swimming body can be described using a traveling wave \cite{Lighthill1969,Lighthill1971},
\begin{equation}
    y(x,t) = (c_1x+c_2x^2)\sin(kx+\omega t),
    \label{eq:ch2_design_wave}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is lateral displacement, $x$ is the position along the body, $c_1$ and $c_2$ define the amplitude envelope, $k$ is the body wave number, and $\omega$ is the oscillation frequency. For a robotic mechanism, the continuous body wave must be approximated by a finite number of rigid links. For a discretized body with link index $i$, the wave description can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        y_i(x_i,t) &= (c_1x_i+c_2x_i^2)\sin(kx_i+\omega t), \\
        l_i &= \sqrt{(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2+(y_{i+1}-y_i)^2}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_discrete_wave}
\end{equation}
This representation motivates the use of at least two effective tail links if the robotic fish is to approximate an undulatory wave rather than a single rigid oscillating foil.

An open-chain two-joint tail could produce this motion by controlling two motors and their relative phase. However, a compact underwater robot benefits from fewer actuators, simpler waterproofing, and simpler low-level control. AquaNavigator therefore uses a double-slider-crank (DSC) mechanism that produces a two-link wave-like motion using one rotary input and a mechanically fixed phase shift \cite{Zuo2021,Masood2025Pipeline}. The phase relationship is selected through the mechanism geometry rather than enforced by two synchronized motors.

The mechanism is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2_dsc_action}. The motor drives two slider-crank stages in series so that the tail links move with a prescribed phase difference. Because the phase difference is set mechanically, the tail can generate an oscillatory wave-like motion without requiring two independently synchronized motors. This is useful for a compact underwater robot because it reduces actuator count and simplifies low-level propulsion commands. The tradeoff is that the tail kinematics are mostly fixed by the mechanism geometry, so the controller regulates swimming behavior through tail frequency and steering bias rather than through arbitrary joint trajectories.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{Fig/ch2_dsc_action.png}
    \caption{Double-slider-crank tail mechanism at four crank positions.}
    \label{fig:ch2_dsc_action}
\end{figure}

Steering is achieved by rotating the complete tail-thrust module with a servo motor, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2_tail_servo}. The servo changes the tail-bias angle, which changes the direction of the average thrust generated by the DSC mechanism. This creates yaw motion and allows the robot to track visual or mission-level commands. The same design also explains why the platform is underactuated: lateral motion appears through hydrodynamic coupling and tail-generated yaw rather than through a separate lateral thruster.

\section{Modeling of the BCD and Robotic Fish}

The modeling framework has two parts. The first part describes the BCD as a coupled buoyancy and actuator system. The second part describes AquaNavigator as a tail-actuated planar robotic fish. The BCD model supports depth-control design, while the robotic-fish model supports simulation, controller tuning, and interpretation of pipeline-tracking experiments.

\subsection{Buoyancy-Device Force Balance}

The BCD model begins with the free-body diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2_bcd_schematic}. Let $x_d$ denote the device depth displacement relative to a neutral reference position, with positive $x_d$ taken downward. The device mass is denoted by $m$, the surrounding water density by $\rho_w$, and the fixed displaced volume at neutral buoyancy by $V_s$. The piston changes the displaced volume by a signed dynamic component $V_d$. The hydrodynamic drag in the vertical direction is approximated as linear with coefficient $c_d$.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{Fig/ch2_bcd_schematic.pdf}
    \caption{BCD free-body model near neutral buoyancy.}
    \label{fig:ch2_bcd_schematic}
\end{figure}

The total displaced volume is
\begin{equation}
    V = V_s + V_d .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_total_volume}
\end{equation}
At the nominal neutral-buoyancy condition,
\begin{equation}
    m = \rho_w V_s .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_neutral}
\end{equation}
The vertical equation of motion can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    m\ddot{x}_d = mg - \rho_w (V_s + V_d)g - c_d\dot{x}_d .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_force_balance}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_bcd_neutral}, the neutral weight and buoyancy terms cancel and the depth dynamics reduce to
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x}_d + \frac{c_d}{m}\dot{x}_d
    = -\frac{\rho_w g}{m}V_d .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_depth_vd}
\end{equation}
The sign of $V_d$ depends on the chosen positive piston direction. In the implementation, the actuator calibration maps command direction to the desired increase or decrease of buoyancy. For modeling, it is convenient to define an effective piston displacement $x_p$ such that
\begin{equation}
    V_d = A_p x_p, \qquad A_p = \pi r_p^2 ,
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_piston_volume}
\end{equation}
where $A_p$ is the piston area and $r_p$ is the piston radius. The piston notation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2_bcd_piston}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{Fig/ch2_bcd_piston.pdf}
    \caption{Piston-cylinder notation for BCD volume change.}
    \label{fig:ch2_bcd_piston}
\end{figure}

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_bcd_piston_volume} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_bcd_depth_vd} gives the basic second-order vertical-motion model,
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x}_d + \frac{c_d}{m}\dot{x}_d
    = -\frac{\rho_w g A_p}{m}x_p .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_depth_xp}
\end{equation}
This equation captures the main physical effect of the BCD: piston displacement changes the displaced volume and therefore creates vertical acceleration. It also shows why the neutral operating point is delicate. If a small piston offset or mass-balancing error creates a nonzero net buoyancy, the device will continue to move unless drag and control action counteract the imbalance.

\subsection{Pressure-Coupled Actuator Model}

The BCD model treats the piston actuator as part of the plant rather than as an ideal position source \cite{Masood2026ESCBCD}. This is important because piston motion is coupled to hydrostatic pressure, internal chamber pressure, friction, backlash, dead-zone behavior, and voltage limits. The motor-side electrical model can be written as
\begin{equation}
    R_a i_a + L_a \dot{i}_a + K_b'\dot{x}_p = u_a ,
    \label{eq:ch2_actuator_electrical}
\end{equation}
where $R_a$ is the armature resistance, $L_a$ is the armature inductance, $i_a$ is the armature current, $K_b'$ is the back-emf constant referred to piston velocity, and $u_a$ is the applied actuator voltage. For the reduced model used in the control development, the armature inductance is neglected, so
\begin{equation}
    i_a = \frac{u_a - K_b'\dot{x}_p}{R_a}.
    \label{eq:ch2_actuator_current}
\end{equation}

The piston-force balance is modeled as
\begin{equation}
    m_l\ddot{x}_p = -f_l\dot{x}_p + K_f i_a + F_p ,
    \label{eq:ch2_piston_force}
\end{equation}
where $m_l$ is the equivalent moving mass of the actuator-piston assembly, $f_l$ is the equivalent viscous damping coefficient, $K_f$ is the actuator force constant, and $F_p$ represents pressure-induced loading on the piston. If pressure terms are temporarily ignored, substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_actuator_current} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_piston_force} gives
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x}_p =
    -\frac{R_a f_l + K_f K_b'}{m_l R_a}\dot{x}_p
    + \frac{K_f}{m_l R_a}u_a .
    \label{eq:ch2_basic_actuator_model}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the reduced actuator model can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \tau \ddot{x}_p + \dot{x}_p = K_l u_a ,
    \label{eq:ch2_tau_actuator}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \tau = \frac{m_l R_a}{R_a f_l + K_f K_b'}, \qquad
    K_l = \frac{K_f}{R_a f_l + K_f K_b'} .
    \label{eq:ch2_tau_kl}
\end{equation}

The pressure-coupled model adds two effects that are important for underwater operation. First, the external hydrostatic pressure changes with depth and loads the piston. Second, the sealed internal volume changes as the piston moves, creating an opposing pressure term. With these effects included, the piston dynamics take the form
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x}_p =
    -\frac{1}{\tau}\dot{x}_p
    + \frac{K_l}{\tau}u_a
    + \frac{A_p \rho_w g}{m_l}x_d
    - \frac{A_p^2 P_s}{m_l V_{se}}x_p ,
    \label{eq:ch2_pressure_coupled_actuator}
\end{equation}
where $P_s$ is the equilibrium internal chamber pressure and $V_{se}$ is the equilibrium enclosed chamber volume. This model shows the coupling between actuator motion and depth. Depth changes the external pressure acting on the piston, while piston motion changes the device volume and therefore the vertical acceleration.

Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch2_bcd_depth_xp} and \eqref{eq:ch2_pressure_coupled_actuator} gives a fourth-order BCD model with state vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_b =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_d & \dot{x}_d & x_p & \dot{x}_p
    \end{bmatrix}^T .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_state}
\end{equation}
A compact state-space form is
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}}_b =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & -\frac{c_d}{m} & -\frac{\rho_w g A_p}{m} & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
        \frac{A_p \rho_w g}{m_l} & 0 &
        -\frac{A_p^2 P_s}{m_l V_{se}} & -\frac{1}{\tau}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{x}_b
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \frac{K_l}{\tau}
    \end{bmatrix}u_a .
    \label{eq:ch2_bcd_statespace}
\end{equation}
The model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch2_bcd_statespace} is the main BCD model used by this dissertation to explain why depth regulation is a constraint-sensitive problem. The controller must respect piston stroke limits, actuator voltage limits, and the instability near neutral buoyancy. These issues are treated in Chapter~3, where model validation and control design are presented.

\subsection{AquaNavigator Dynamic Model}

The robotic-fish dynamic model is derived in two steps. First, the force generated by the flapping DSC tail is estimated. Second, the planar rigid-body dynamics of the robot are written using surge, sway, and yaw motion. The model represents the dominant propulsion and planar-motion effects needed for simulation, controller design, and interpretation of the experimental behavior.

The coordinate frames used for the planar model are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2_robot_frames}:
\begin{itemize}
\item[$\{W\}$] Fixed to the laboratory or simulation environment
\item[$\{B\}$] Attached to the center of mass of the robotic fish
\item[$\{D\}$] Attached to the tail mechanism
\item[$\{CF\}$] Attached to the tail/fin section used for force estimation
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig/ch2_robot_frames.png}
    \caption{AquaNavigator frames for propulsion and planar dynamics.}
    \label{fig:ch2_robot_frames}
\end{figure}

The world frame is placed on the water-surface plane, with its $z$-axis pointing downward. For tank experiments, its origin can be chosen at a convenient fixed point in the camera view. The body frame uses the robot heading direction as its $x$-axis and the lateral direction as its $y$-axis. The DSC frame is located at the tail mechanism joint associated with the link $l_0$, and the caudal-fin frame is placed at the quarter-chord point of the fin. The caudal-fin frame is used because the elongated-body force approximation is evaluated from the normal and tangential velocities at that point.

The following assumptions are used in the planar model:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Roll and pitch motion are assumed to be negligible because the robot is balanced so that it remains upright during nominal swimming.
\item The robotic fish is treated as an underactuated nonholonomic system. Sway appears through hydrodynamic coupling rather than independent lateral thrust.
\item The body-fixed frame is located at the center of mass, with surge velocity $u$, sway velocity $v$, and yaw rate $r$.
\item The surrounding fluid is treated using a simplified hydrodynamic approximation, so the model focuses on dominant inertial and drag effects rather than full CFD.
\item The caudal-fin force is estimated using an elongated-body-theory approximation, with the tail treated as a stiff, low-frequency oscillating structure.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Tail Kinematics and Thrust Estimation}

Let $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ denote the angular positions of the two effective tail links. For the DSC mechanism, the link motion is approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \alpha_1(t) &= A_1 \sin(\omega t), \\
        \alpha_2(t) &= A_2 \sin(\omega t+\phi_{dsc}),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_dsc_angles}
\end{equation}
where $A_1$ and $A_2$ are link angular amplitudes and $\phi_{dsc}$ is the fixed phase difference set by the mechanism geometry. The main propulsion command is the tail oscillation frequency $\omega$, while the steering command is the tail-bias angle $\delta$. Following the DSC mechanism design, $\phi_{dsc}$ is taken as a fixed phase difference.

If the quarter-chord point of the caudal fin is used as the force-evaluation point, its position in the DSC frame can be approximated as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_{qc} &= l_1\cos(\alpha_1) + l_2\cos(\alpha_2), \\
        y_{qc} &= l_1\sin(\alpha_1) + l_2\sin(\alpha_2), \\
        \dot{x}_{qc} &= -l_1\omega\sin(\alpha_1)-l_2\omega\sin(\alpha_2), \\
        \dot{y}_{qc} &= l_1\omega\cos(\alpha_1)+l_2\omega\cos(\alpha_2),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_qc_position}
\end{equation}
where $l_1$ and $l_2$ are effective link lengths. The corresponding normal and tangential velocity components in the caudal-fin frame are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        V_{\parallel} &=
        -\dot{x}_{qc}\sin(\alpha_2) + \dot{y}_{qc}\cos(\alpha_2), \\
        V_{\perp} &=
        \dot{x}_{qc}\cos(\alpha_2) + \dot{y}_{qc}\sin(\alpha_2).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_cf_velocity}
\end{equation}
Using the elongated-body approximation adopted in the pipeline-tracking model \cite{Wang2013,Lighthill1971,Masood2025Pipeline}, the tail-generated force in the DSC frame is written in terms of the normal and tangential velocity components as
\begin{equation}
    {}^{D}\mathbf{F}
    =
    \frac{1}{2}m_i V_{\perp}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{m}}
    - m_i V_{\perp}V_{\parallel}\hat{\mathbf{n}}
    + m_i L\dot{V}_{\perp}\hat{\mathbf{n}},
    \label{eq:ch2_tail_force_d}
\end{equation}
where $m_i$ is the virtual mass per unit length, $L$ is an effective body length, and $\hat{\mathbf{m}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ are the tangential and normal unit directions used in the tail-force approximation. The virtual mass per unit length is represented as
\begin{equation}
    m_i = \frac{1}{4}\pi\rho_w h^2 ,
    \label{eq:ch2_virtual_mass}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the submerged body height used in the elongated-body approximation. The effective length $L$ is computed from the measured body and tail geometry of the prototype. The tail-bias angle $\delta$ rotates the generated force into the body frame:
\begin{equation}
    {}^{B}\mathbf{F}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        F_x \\ F_y
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos\delta & -\sin\delta \\
        \sin\delta & \cos\delta
    \end{bmatrix}
    {}^{D}\mathbf{F}.
    \label{eq:ch2_tail_force_body}
\end{equation}
The resulting yaw moment about the body center is expressed as
\begin{equation}
    F_{\theta}
    =
    -\left((L-d_0)\cos\delta\right)F_y
    + (l_0+l_1+l_2)\sin\delta\,F_x ,
    \label{eq:ch2_yaw_moment}
\end{equation}
where $d_0$ and $l_0$ are geometry parameters associated with the body and tail-module placement.

\subsubsection{Planar Rigid-Body Dynamics}

The planar swimming model treats AquaNavigator as an underactuated surface or near-surface vehicle with surge, sway, and yaw dynamics. Let
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\nu} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u & v & r
    \end{bmatrix}^T
    \label{eq:ch2_body_velocity}
\end{equation}
denote the body-frame velocity vector, where $u$ is surge velocity, $v$ is sway velocity, and $r$ is yaw rate. The general rigid-body form is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M}\dot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
    + \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu}
    + \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\nu}
    =
    \mathbf{F},
    \label{eq:ch2_general_fish_model}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the inertia and added-mass matrix, $\mathbf{C}$ is the Coriolis and centripetal matrix, $\mathbf{D}$ is the damping matrix, and
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        F_x & F_y & F_{\theta}
    \end{bmatrix}^T
    \label{eq:ch2_fish_force_vector}
\end{equation}
is the generalized force vector generated primarily by the tail mechanism.

Using the simplified form from the pipeline-tracking model, the planar equations can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        (m+k_{11}m)\dot{u}
        &= F_x + (m+k_{22}m)vr - D_u u, \\
        (m+k_{22}m)\dot{v}
        &= F_y + (m+k_{11}m)ur - D_v v, \\
        (I+k_{55}I)\dot{r}
        &= F_{\theta} - (k_{22}m-k_{11}m)uv - D_r r .
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_planar_eom}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is the robot mass, $I$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $k_{11}$, $k_{22}$, and $k_{55}$ are added-mass coefficients, and $D_u$, $D_v$, and $D_r$ are lumped damping coefficients. The added-mass approximation treats the body as a prolate ellipsoid with semi-axes $a$, $b$, and $b$.

The added-mass coefficients are estimated using Lamb's $k$-factor representation,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        k_{11} &= \frac{\alpha_0}{2-\alpha_0}, \\
        k_{22} &= \frac{\beta_0}{2-\beta_0}, \\
        k_{55} &=
        \frac{e^4(\beta_0-\alpha_0)}
        {(2-e^2)\left(2e^2-(2-e^2)(\beta_0-\alpha_0)\right)},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_added_mass_coefficients}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \alpha_0 &=
        \frac{2(1-e^2)}{e^3}
        \left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+e}{1-e}-e\right), \\
        \beta_0 &=
        \frac{1}{e^2}
        -\frac{1-e^2}{2e^3}\ln\frac{1+e}{1-e},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_lamb_factors}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    e = \sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}}
    \label{eq:ch2_eccentricity}
\end{equation}
is the ellipsoid eccentricity. The ellipsoid approximation is chosen so that the body mass and yaw inertia are consistent with
\begin{equation}
    m = \frac{4}{3}\pi\rho_wab^2, \qquad
    I = \frac{4}{15}\pi\rho_wab^2(a^2+b^2).
    \label{eq:ch2_ellipsoid_mass_inertia}
\end{equation}
For AquaNavigator, this approximation requires care because some body cavities and passive covers can fill with water during operation. The added-mass approximation depends on the effective submerged geometry together with the dry mass.

The damping matrix is written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        D_u & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & D_v & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & D_r
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial u} & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial v} & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & \frac{\partial F_{\theta}}{\partial r}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:ch2_damping_matrix}
\end{equation}
The nonlinear drag components can be approximated as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F_{D_u} &= \frac{1}{2}\rho_w A_u C_u |u|u, \\
        F_{D_v} &= \frac{1}{2}\rho_w A_v C_v |v|v, \\
        F_{D_r} &= C_r |r|r,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_drag_forces}
\end{equation}
where $A_u$, $A_v$, and $A_r$ are reference areas and $C_u$, $C_v$, and $C_r$ are drag coefficients. A local linear damping approximation can be taken around nominal velocities:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        D_u(\bar{u}) &=
        \left.\frac{\partial F_{D_u}}{\partial u}\right|_{u=\bar{u}}, \\
        D_v(\bar{v}) &=
        \left.\frac{\partial F_{D_v}}{\partial v}\right|_{v=\bar{v}}, \\
        D_r(\bar{r}) &=
        \left.\frac{\partial F_{D_r}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=\bar{r}}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch2_linear_damping}
\end{equation}
The resulting model is the basis for the simulation environment used in the pipeline-tracking work \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}. Table~\ref{tab:ch2_fish_model_parameters} lists the experimental and calculated parameters used in the model.

\section{Model Validation of the BCD}

The BCD model in Chapter~2 couples vertical motion with piston-actuator dynamics. Before using that model for controller design, its dominant behavior must be checked against hardware measurements. The validation procedure uses an open-loop experiment with the standalone BCD \cite{Masood2026ESCBCD}. The validation evaluates whether the model reproduces the main actuator response and the resulting depth response well enough to support controller design and to identify where unmodeled effects remain.

The validation experiment is performed on the standalone BCD, also referred to as DiveSync. The actuator has built-in self-locking up to approximately $50$~N of holding force. For the tested depth range, the hydrostatic load is below this threshold, so the actuator holds its position between drive commands. This makes an open-loop validation meaningful over the tested operating range.

In the experiment, a square-wave voltage input $u(t)$ is applied to the linear actuator. The actuator position $z_a(t)$ and the device depth $z_d(t)$ are recorded simultaneously. The same input is then applied to the model, and the simulated actuator and depth responses are compared with the measured responses. The comparison is later shown in the results section. This validation method is useful because it separates two parts of the model: the actuator subsystem is checked through $z_a(t)$, while the buoyancy/depth subsystem is checked through $z_d(t)$.

\section{MPC-Based BCD Control Design}

The first closed-loop BCD controller considered in this dissertation is a constrained model predictive controller (MPC) \cite{Masood2023}. MPC is appropriate for the BCD because actuator motion is physically bounded. A command beyond the available stroke saturates the piston and limits the achievable buoyancy change. Near neutral buoyancy, this loss of control authority can cause persistent drift or unstable depth behavior. The MPC formulation addresses this by optimizing depth tracking while explicitly respecting the piston-position constraint.

The MPC objective is written as a finite-horizon quadratic cost,
\begin{equation}
    J(t_0) =
    \sum_{t=t_0}^{t_0+T}
    \left[
        \left(C\mathbf{x}(t)-r(t)\right)^T
        Q
        \left(C\mathbf{x}(t)-r(t)\right)
        +
        \mathbf{u}(t)^T R \mathbf{u}(t)
    \right],
    \label{eq:ch3_mpc_cost}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the model state, $C\mathbf{x}$ is the predicted depth output, $r$ is the reference depth, $Q$ penalizes tracking error, and $R$ penalizes control effort. The controller is subject to the hard actuator bound
\begin{equation}
    |u| \leq u_b ,
    \label{eq:ch3_mpc_constraint}
\end{equation}
where $u_b$ is the maximum admissible piston command. For the BCD experiments, the bound is $u_b=20$~mm. After the constrained optimization is solved, the first control move is applied and the horizon is shifted forward.

For the implemented controller, the plant is discretized with a sampling time of $0.1$~s. The prediction horizon is $80$ steps, the control horizon is $20$ steps, and the weights are set to $Q=1$ and $R=40$. Only the depth state is directly measured by the depth sensor. The remaining states are estimated by the internal observer in the MPC implementation, so the controller can use full-state feedback in the optimization. The control structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3_mpc_control_scheme}.

\section{Extremum Seeking Control of the BCD}

The MPC design explicitly handles constraints, but its performance depends on the accuracy of the plant model. The fabricated BCD includes actuator dead zone, backlash, friction, direction-dependent behavior, pressure loading, sensor noise, and disturbances from cable tension and tank-wall effects. These effects are difficult to capture exactly in a fixed model. The ESC-based BCD controller therefore uses extremum seeking control (ESC) as an online adaptation layer for depth regulation \cite{Masood2026ESCBCD}.

The BCD has four main dynamic states: depth, depth velocity, piston position, and piston velocity. Depth and piston position are measured directly, while the velocity states are estimated. The control input is actuator voltage, and both voltage and piston stroke are bounded. A depth-only PID controller can reduce depth error while still driving the piston toward a stroke limit. The ESC design therefore treats controller tuning as an online optimization problem shaped by physical constraints.

Classical ESC can be understood locally by considering an unknown static map $y=f(\theta)$ near an extremum $\theta^\ast$:
\begin{equation}
    f(\theta)
    \approx
    f(\theta^\ast)
    + \frac{1}{2}f''(\theta^\ast)(\theta-\theta^\ast)^2 .
    \label{eq:ch3_esc_static_map}
\end{equation}
A sinusoidal perturbation is added to the decision variable, and the measured response is high-pass filtered, demodulated, low-pass filtered, and integrated to update the estimate of the improving parameter. The BCD depth response is dynamic and trajectory-dependent, so three ESC structures were considered.

The first structure applies ESC directly to the actuator voltage. This is useful as a baseline because voltage first changes piston motion and the depth dynamics then evolve from the resulting buoyancy change. The second structure adds an inner actuator-position loop. This improves actuator behavior, while the third structure adds the depth stabilization used for the main ESC results: an ESC-tuned PD depth controller. Its inner depth-control law is
\begin{equation}
    u = K_p(r-z) - K_d \dot{z},
    \label{eq:ch3_esc_pd_law}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the depth reference, $z$ is measured depth, and $\dot{z}$ is estimated depth velocity. The ESC layer adjusts the effective gains online instead of fixing them manually. The proposed architecture is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3_esc_tuned_pd_scheme}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{Fig/ch3_esc_tuned_pd_scheme.png}
    \caption{ESC-tuned PD controller architecture for BCD.}
    \label{fig:ch3_esc_tuned_pd_scheme}
\end{figure}

Two cost functions are important. The first penalizes actuator motion:
\begin{equation}
    J = \dot{x}^2 ,
    \label{eq:ch3_esc_velocity_cost}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is piston displacement. This encourages reduced actuator activity. The second cost adds a deadzone penalty around the neutral piston position so that piston motion near the stroke limits is penalized more strongly:
\begin{equation}
    J_z =
    w_z
    \left[
        \max\left(0,\left|z_a-z_{a,\mathrm{eq}}\right|-\delta_z\right)
    \right]^2 ,
    \label{eq:ch3_esc_deadzone_cost}
\end{equation}
where $z_a$ is piston position, $z_{a,\mathrm{eq}}$ is the neutral piston position, $\delta_z$ is the safe-band half-width, and $w_z$ is the penalty weight. When the piston remains inside the safe band, this penalty is zero. When it moves toward a stroke limit, the penalty activates and drives adaptation toward gains that pull the piston back toward the middle of the stroke.

For the hardware experiment, the cost combines depth tracking with the deadzone penalty and a piston-velocity term:
\begin{equation}
    J =
    (r-\hat{z})^2
    +
    w_z
    \left[
        \max\left(0,\left|z_a-z_{a,\mathrm{eq}}\right|-\delta_z\right)
    \right]^2
    +
    w_{\dot{z}}\dot{z}_a^2 .
    \label{eq:ch3_esc_experimental_cost}
\end{equation}
The first term improves depth tracking, the second term protects the piston stroke margin, and the third term discourages unnecessary actuator motion. In the implemented experiment, the derivative gain is tied to the proportional gain by $K_d=T_DK_p$, so one ESC channel adapts $\hat{K}_p$ and the derivative gain follows through the fixed time constant $T_D$.

\section{Pipeline Tracking Control of AquaNavigator}

The pipeline-tracking controller uses AquaNavigator as a camera-guided planar swimming robot \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}. In this chapter, pipeline tracking is treated as a control-design problem for the robotic fish. Its inspection relevance carries into the next chapter through the oil-spill experiment, where pipeline tracking provides the path-following behavior for mobile sensing. The control objective is to align the fish with a pipeline-like feature detected in the camera image and reduce the lateral image-space error. These experiments evaluate planar pipeline tracking, while depth regulation is handled separately by the BCD work.

The onboard camera is mounted near the front of the robot and tilted downward. The image stream is sent to a host computer where OpenCV-based processing converts the frame to grayscale, applies edge detection, and uses a Hough-transform pipeline to identify the dominant line corresponding to the pipeline. The detected pipeline is represented as an image-space vector $\vec{P}$. The robot heading is represented by a heading vector $\vec{h}$ fixed in the image frame because the camera is fixed relative to the robot body. The tracking error is an angle $\psi_e$ formed from these vectors. The controller drives $\psi_e$ toward zero so the robot becomes aligned with the detected pipeline.

The visual-servoing control structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3_pipeline_control_scheme}. The controller uses a PID law to command the tail-bias angle:
\begin{equation}
    \delta(i+1)
    =
    K_p\psi_e(i)
    +
    K_i\sum_{j=i-w_i}^{i}\psi_e(j)\Delta t
    +
    K_d\frac{\psi_e(i)-\psi_e(i-1)}{\Delta t}.
    \label{eq:ch3_pipeline_pid}
\end{equation}
The tail-beat frequency is held constant during the tracking experiment, while $\delta$ changes the direction of the average tail-generated thrust. The commanded tail-bias angle is saturated at $\pm 20^\circ$ in the embedded safety logic. This saturation corresponds to the practical turning limit of the prototype; experiments with the saturated command indicated a turning radius of approximately $3$~m.

\section{Robotic Fish Simulator}
\label{sec:ch3_robotic_fish_simulator}

The simulator is used to test the robotic-fish model and visual-servo control before pool experiments. It numerically integrates the planar dynamic model from Chapter~2 using a fixed time step and represents the water tank, pipeline, and robotic fish as modular components. The simulator stores state and control histories so that controller gains, tracking errors, and trajectory behavior can be evaluated before operating the physical robot.

For the pipeline-tracking experiment, the simulated tank is $10$~m long and $5$~m wide, with a pipeline placed on the bottom surface. The robot is initialized $0.5$~m away from the pipeline, and the tail-beat frequency is set to $11$~rad/s. The simulation is run for $35$~s with a time step of $0.05$~s. Because tail-driven propulsion produces head and body oscillation, the raw image-space tracking angle contains high-frequency motion. A moving-average filter with a $15$-sample window is applied to $\psi_e$. At a sampling frequency of $20$~Hz, this corresponds to a smoothing window of $0.75$~s and an approximate cutoff frequency of $0.67$~Hz.

Both position-based visual servoing (PBVS) and image-based visual servoing (IBVS) are tested in the simulator. PBVS uses world-frame position and orientation information available in simulation. IBVS uses image-space quantities, which better matches the physical experiment because the robot relies on its onboard camera for feedback. For this reason, IBVS is the preferred implementation for the pool experiment.

The physical experiments in this chapter use two controlled laboratory platforms. \subsection{BCD Model-Validation Setup}
\label{sec:ch3_bcd_validation_setup}

The BCD model-validation experiment uses the standalone buoyancy device rather than the full robotic fish. The piston-cylinder assembly is driven by an Actuonix L12 miniature linear actuator with integrated position feedback. An ESP32-C3 embedded controller drives the actuator through a motor-driver circuit, and an LPS33HW pressure sensor provides depth feedback. Communication with the host computer is through a USB serial connection, with a Python host program used for command generation, telemetry visualization, and synchronized logging of actuator position, command activity, and depth/pressure data.

For open-loop validation, the device is tested in a laboratory water tank. A square-wave voltage input is applied to the linear actuator while the actuator position $z_a(t)$ and device depth $z_d(t)$ are recorded. The purpose of this experiment is to compare the measured actuator and depth responses against the BCD model from Chapter~2.

\subsection{MPC Depth-Control Setup}
\label{sec:ch3_mpc_experimental_setup}

The MPC experiment uses the same class of standalone buoyancy-control hardware, but with the controller implemented through a MATLAB host computer. Before the depth-control run, the device is sealed, checked for water ingress at $50$~cm depth for three minutes, adjusted to neutral buoyancy with dead weights, and balanced so that it remains vertically oriented. The onboard ESP32-C3 controller, motor driver, pressure sensor, and self-contained power electronics remain inside the device, while the host computer communicates with the device over a wireless UDP link.

In this setup, the depth sensor provides the measured output to the host-side MPC. MATLAB implements the Kalman estimator and state observer, computes the constrained control action, and transmits the command back to the embedded controller. The plant discretization and controller update use a fixed sampling time of $0.1$~s. The MPC experiment uses the same controller settings as the simulation in this chapter, including $Q=1$, $R=40$, a prediction horizon of $80$ steps, and a control horizon of $20$ steps. The physical validation uses a $35$~mm depth step command.

\subsection{ESC Depth-Control Setup}
\label{sec:ch3_esc_experimental_setup}

The ESC experiments use the updated BCD test setup with USB serial telemetry and the Python host interface. The embedded controller streams actuator position, pressure/depth data, and command feedback to the host computer. The host interface logs the data, displays the experiment in real time, and sends actuator commands back to the embedded controller. This arrangement is used first to check the actuator-position loop before closing the outer depth-control loop.

Two hardware stages are therefore distinguished. The actuator-loop validation stage commands piston-position changes and checks whether the actuator can track the requested setpoints repeatably despite dead zone, backlash, and measurement jitter. The depth-loop ESC stage then uses the measured depth response to adapt the PD gains online. During the depth experiment, the voltage command is constrained within $\pm 12$~V and the piston is required to remain within its $[0,50]$~mm physical stroke. Cable tension, actuator dead zone, and tank-wall effects are treated as experimental disturbances rather than modeled inputs.

\subsection{Pipeline-Tracking Pool Setup}
\label{sec:ch3_pipeline_experimental_setup}

The pipeline-tracking experiment uses AquaNavigator in an indoor portable water tank that is $10$~m long and $5$~m wide. A pipeline-like feature is placed on the bottom of the tank. A roof-mounted camera records the robot motion from the top view for post-experiment analysis. Feedback control relies on the onboard forward-facing camera mounted inside the robot enclosure, with the camera tilted $30^\circ$ downward from the horizon \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}.

The robot is connected to a host computer through USB. An onboard USB hub separates the camera and microcontroller connections, while the custom PCB integrates power distribution, motor drivers, the microcontroller interface, and connector routing. The robot is powered by a $1500$~mAh 3S LiPo battery. The embedded controller handles low-level actuation, safety behavior, and actuator commands, while the host computer runs the Python GUI used to configure manual operation and pipeline-tracking missions. During these pool experiments, depth control is inactive; only the planar pipeline-tracking controller is evaluated.

The host-side Python program receives the onboard camera stream and processes each frame using OpenCV. The image is converted to grayscale, filtered through Canny edge detection, and processed with a Hough-transform line detector. The dominant detected line is treated as the pipeline. The program computes the image-space heading error $\psi_e$ and lateral error $y$, then sends the corresponding tail-bias command to the microcontroller. For each trial, the software logs the experiment identifier, timestamp, $\psi_e$, and $y$ to a \texttt{.csv} file for post-processing. The camera-vector definition used in this setup is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3_pipeline_camera_vectors}, and the pool result is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3_pipeline_top_view} and Table~\ref{tab:ch3_pipeline_results}.

\section{Conclusion}
The open-loop BCD validation gave an actuator-position RMSE of $1.96$~mm and a depth-response RMSE of $23.07$~mm, showing that the identified actuator dynamics were captured more tightly than the coupled depth response. The remaining depth error is consistent with unmodeled drag variation, tank-wall effects, and small neutral-buoyancy offsets in the laboratory setup. First an MPC controller was developed that used the model to enforce piston constraints and produced acceptable depth regulation while respecting actuator limits, but its performance depended on model fidelity. The ESC-tuned PD controller addressed this limitation by adapting gains online from measured performance while respecting voltage and stroke limits.

For the robotic fish, image-based visual servoing was used to track a pipeline-like feature by commanding the tail-bias angle, with the simulator supporting PBVS and IBVS comparison and gain tuning before pool experiments. The results show that the BCD and AquaNavigator can be controlled in constrained laboratory and pool settings. Pipeline tracking is a first inspection behavior, and depth regulation is a prerequisite for three-dimensional operation.

\chapter{Safety, Inspection, and Monitoring of Underwater Infrastructure}

The previous chapters established the physical platform, the buoyancy-control model, and the control methods needed for depth regulation and pipeline-following motion. Infrastructure monitoring also requires sensors, mobile data acquisition, and methods that convert measurements into interpretable information about the surrounding environment. This chapter therefore shifts from robot design and control to environmental sensing with AquaNavigator.

Subsea monitoring includes path following, depth regulation, and measurement of environmental changes around infrastructure. A leak, spill, or chemical release can move away from its source, mix with water, appear intermittently, or produce a spatial pattern that depends on flow, depth, sensor response time, and robot trajectory. Fixed sensors can provide long-term measurements at selected locations, while a mobile robotic platform can sample across a region of interest and connect each measurement to the robot's position or path.

Two sensing studies are presented in this chapter. The first uses a near-infrared \mbox{CO$_2$} sensor and position-tagged measurements to estimate the source of a controlled underwater \mbox{CO$_2$} leak \cite{Masood2025CO2}. The second integrates an organic electrochemical transistor (OECT) sensor with AquaNavigator for crude-oil detection during pipeline-following experiments \cite{Masood2026OilSpill}. The \mbox{CO$_2$} study emphasizes spatial source localization, while the oil-spill study emphasizes chemical-sensor packaging, signal conditioning, and detection feasibility on a moving robotic fish.

\section{AquaNavigator as a Mobile Sensing Platform}

AquaNavigator is used in this chapter as a common mobile sensing platform for underwater environmental monitoring. Rather than treating each experiment as a separate robotic system, the platform is considered as a reusable carrier that can support different sensing payloads, data-acquisition requirements, and operating conditions. Its sealed enclosure, onboard electronics, modular payload space, and tail-driven mobility allow the robot to carry sensors through a controlled water environment and collect measurements at different locations.

The common idea across the sensing studies is spatial sampling. A sensor payload is mounted on the robotic fish and operated while the platform interacts with the surrounding water. In the \mbox{CO$_2$} experiment, the objective is to measure dissolved carbon dioxide concentration and associate the measurements with the robot's position in the tank. In the oil-spill experiment, the objective is to measure the OECT drain-source current response as the sensor encounters clean and contaminated water conditions. Although the two sensing mechanisms are different, both experiments use the robotic fish as a mobile platform for collecting environmental information.

The same practical constraints apply to both studies. Sensor response time, position-estimation accuracy, and tank-scale disturbances define how the measurements are interpreted. The goal of this chapter is therefore to evaluate AquaNavigator as a modular underwater sensing platform and to identify the technical steps needed before extending the approach to field-scale monitoring.

\section[CO2 Leak Source Localization]{CO$_2$ Leak Source Localization}

\subsection{Motivation and Sensing Strategy}

Carbon capture and storage creates a monitoring problem after \mbox{CO$_2$} has been captured and transported. A subsea \mbox{CO$_2$} leak may dissolve into seawater, form carbonic acid, lower pH, and affect nearby marine organisms \cite{Herzog2001,Filonchyk2024,Kabir2023,Zheng2021,Pham2016}. For this reason, monitoring combines mechanical pipeline inspection with chemical measurements of the surrounding water.

The sensing strategy is to use AquaNavigator as a mobile carrier for a near-infrared \mbox{CO$_2$} sensor. The sensor reports \mbox{CO$_2$} concentration while the host computer associates each measurement with a planar position in the water tank. Because GPS is unavailable underwater and the experiment is indoors, an overhead RGB camera provides the laboratory position reference for evaluating source estimation with position-tagged measurements.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Data Collection}

Two \mbox{CO$_2$} experiments are used. First, a diffusion test is performed in a 1.2~m $\times$ 1.2~m $\times$ 1.2~m tank using a \mbox{CO$_2$} cylinder, regulator, flow meter, and submerged pipe with a small hole at the bottom. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_co2_diffusion_setup} shows this setup. The leak flow rate is set to 1~LPM. The sensor baseline is recorded before the leak is turned on, and measurements are taken after 5~min stabilization intervals over a total duration of 30~min. The measurements are collected at depths from 0.1~m to 0.4~m below the water surface and horizontal offsets from 0~m to 0.3~m from the leak point.

    \caption{CO$_2$ diffusion test setup in water.}
    \label{fig:ch4_co2_diffusion_setup}
\end{figure}

Second, the robotic source-localization experiment is conducted in a 10~m $\times$ 5~m $\times$ 2~m indoor water tank filled to about 70\% of its height with fresh water. An overhead RGB camera covers approximately 8~m $\times$ 6~m of the tank. A \mbox{CO$_2$} cylinder and regulator feed a submerged 2~in pipe with a small hole at the bottom to simulate a leak source. The actual source location used for evaluation is $(3.0,3.5)$~m in the tank coordinate frame. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_co2_experimental_setup} shows the complete tank setup.

\begin{figure}[t]

    \label{fig:ch4_co2_experimental_setup}
\end{figure}

During the robotic experiment, AquaNavigator is manually operated through the host software while the \mbox{CO$_2$} sensor streams concentration measurements. The overhead camera initially detects the robot with human assistance, and the host software then tracks its position using optical-flow-based tracking. Each concentration sample is tagged with the corresponding tank-plane position and timestamp. The resulting position-tagged measurements are analyzed in Sec.~\ref{sec:ch4_results_discussion}.

\subsection{Localization Method}

The raw dataset is represented as $(x_n,y_n,C_n)$, where $(x_n,y_n)$ is the planar position of the robot and $C_n$ is the measured \mbox{CO$_2$} concentration. Although the data are collected over time at approximately 0.1~s intervals, the sensor response time is on the order of 60~s. The analysis therefore treats the measurements over short windows as quasi-steady spatial samples for source estimation.

The first representation is a binned heatmap. For a grid cell centered at $(x_i,y_j)$, the mean concentration is computed as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{C}(x_i,y_j) = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N} C_n \delta_{ij}(x_n,y_n)}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \delta_{ij}(x_n,y_n)}.
    \label{eq:ch4_co2_binned_heatmap}
\end{equation}
Here, $\delta_{ij}$ assigns a sample to the grid cell when the sample position lies inside that cell. The same measurements are also interpolated onto a regular grid. Linear interpolation is used inside the convex hull of measured points, and nearest-neighbor filling is used outside the hull. An RBF field is then computed as
\begin{equation}
    C_r(x_i,y_j) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n \phi\left(\left\|(x_i,y_j)-(x_n,y_n)\right\|\right),
    \label{eq:ch4_co2_rbf}
\end{equation}
where the Gaussian basis is
\begin{equation}
    \phi(r) = \exp\left[-\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^2\right].
    \label{eq:ch4_co2_gaussian_rbf}
\end{equation}
Local maxima in the interpolated field are treated as candidate leak locations. The highest three peaks are averaged to estimate the source. The same source-estimation process is repeated over cumulative 75~s windows to evaluate how the estimate changes as more measurements are collected.

\section{Oil Spill Detection}

\subsection{Motivation and OECT Sensing Principle}

Oil-spill monitoring includes both leak detection near subsea infrastructure and environmental contamination assessment after oil enters the surrounding water. Offshore production and subsea transport remain important parts of the energy system \cite{HeinrichBoellStiftung2017}. Crude-oil release can harm marine organisms, seabirds, coastal ecosystems, and human activity near affected regions \cite{Almeda2014,Albers1982}. A mobile robot can support this problem by carrying a sensor close to the suspected leak path instead of relying only on remote imagery or internal pipeline measurements.

The sensing element used here is an OECT sensor previously demonstrated for crude-oil detection in aqueous environments \cite{Porter2024}. The device uses a PEDOT:PSS channel coated with a hydrophobic polystyrene layer. When oil contacts the coated channel, the ionic permeability of the channel changes, increasing impedance and reducing the source-drain current. In the reported laboratory characterization, the sensor can detect oil at concentrations down to 10~$\mu$g and can operate in water-based media, including seawater.

\subsection{Signal Conditioning and Platform Integration}

Laboratory source-measurement equipment is too large and power-demanding for robotic use, so a compact signal-conditioning circuit is used for the OECT payload. The circuit supplies the gate excitation, maintains the drain-source bias, measures the drain-source current, and communicates with the host computer. The final circuit provides a variable gate-voltage output, a fixed drain-voltage supply, and a current-sensing input. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_oect_circuit} shows the circuit architecture.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{Fig/ch4_oect_circuit.png}
    \caption{OECT signal-conditioning circuit for oil detection.}
    \label{fig:ch4_oect_circuit}
\end{figure}

The circuit and sensor are packaged in a waterproof enclosure with sealed cable ports, an O-ring interface, and a sensor-facing cap, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_oect_enclosure}. Because crude oil is hydrophobic and less dense than water, it tends to rise and accumulate near the air-water interface. The OECT module is therefore mounted on top of AquaNavigator to increase the likelihood of contact with surface-level oil contamination.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{Fig/ch4_oect_enclosure.png}
    \caption{Waterproof OECT enclosure for robotic integration.}
    \label{fig:ch4_oect_enclosure}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Detection Method}

The oil experiments have two stages. The first stage characterizes the OECT sensor in the laboratory using both source-measurement equipment and the compact signal-conditioning circuit. The gate-voltage window is selected from $-0.6$~V to $+0.6$~V to obtain a measurable response while limiting water electrolysis, and a constant drain-source bias of $-0.6$~V is applied. The sensor is tested in pool water, seawater, direct oil exposure, and oil-contaminated water.

The second stage integrates the OECT sensor with AquaNavigator in an indoor water tank. The tank is approximately 10~m long, 5~m wide, and 1.5~m deep, and a black pipeline-like feature is placed along the bottom. The robotic fish follows the pipeline using the camera-based tracking behavior described in Chapter~3 while the host computer records the OECT response. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_oil_fish_pool} shows the integrated robotic setup.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{Fig/ch4_oil_fish_pool.png}
    \caption{Oil-sensing AquaNavigator during pipeline pool experiments.}
    \label{fig:ch4_oil_fish_pool}
\end{figure}

Oil detection is based on attenuation of the square-wave pulse response. For each gate-voltage pulse window, the current amplitude is computed as
\begin{equation}
    A_k = I_{\mathrm{high},k} - I_{\mathrm{low},k}.
    \label{eq:ch4_oil_pulse_amplitude}
\end{equation}
The average pulse response over $N$ windows is compared with the clean-water baseline amplitude $A_0$ using
\begin{equation}
    S = 1 - \frac{1}{A_0}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} A_k\right).
    \label{eq:ch4_oil_attenuation}
\end{equation}
When $S$ exceeds a selected threshold, the host-side routine classifies the measurement as oil-contaminated. The experimental results from the laboratory and robotic tests are analyzed in Sec.~\ref{sec:ch4_results_discussion}.

The oil results show that the sensor response is separable across clean, direct-oil, oil-contaminated, and robotic pool conditions. They also show the integration limits. The experiment demonstrates detection feasibility on AquaNavigator, while the response time means that the robot path and residence time in the contaminated region affect localization accuracy. Wave, current, sediment-resuspension, biological-fouling, and debris cases remain important future validation conditions.

\subsection{System-Level Discussion}

The \mbox{CO$_2$} and oil-spill studies use different sensing principles. The \mbox{CO$_2$} work uses a concentration sensor and spatial data processing to estimate a source region. The oil work uses an OECT chemical sensor and current attenuation to detect direct or local contact with oil contamination. Despite the difference in sensor physics, both studies use the same system idea: AquaNavigator carries a payload, moves through a controlled underwater environment, and connects sensor data to robot motion.

The two studies also emphasize different parts of the environmental-monitoring problem. The \mbox{CO$_2$} experiment emphasizes source localization from spatially distributed measurements. Its main result is the reduction of source-estimation error to 0.69~m in the controlled tank dataset. The oil experiment emphasizes integration of a laboratory chemical sensor into a robotic payload. Its main result is the separation between clean-water and contaminated-water responses during pipeline-following experiments, including the observed 60~s response delay and the stronger attenuation after longer exposure.

The shared limitations define the next technical steps. Both studies are controlled tank or pool experiments with host-side computation and laboratory localization support. The \mbox{CO$_2$} analysis treats short windows as quasi-steady, so future work should include current, bubble-dynamics, and sensor-lag modeling. The oil experiment should also be extended to waves, sediment, fouling, and debris. Together, these needs motivate improved onboard localization, stronger onboard autonomy, sensor-response compensation, and testing under more realistic underwater disturbances.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter presented AquaNavigator as a mobile sensing platform for subsea environmental monitoring. The \mbox{CO$_2$} study showed how position-tagged concentration measurements can be processed through heatmaps, interpolation, peak detection, and time-windowed analysis to localize a controlled leak source. The oil-spill study showed how an OECT sensor can be packaged, conditioned, integrated with the robotic fish, and used to detect oil contamination during pipeline-following experiments. Together, the two studies extend the thesis from robot design and control toward robotic environmental monitoring. Chapter~5 builds on this direction by addressing relative localization, which is needed for future cooperative monitoring by multiple underwater robots.
\chapter{RSS-Based Relative Localization for Underwater Robotic Swarms}

The previous chapters considered a single robotic fish as a platform for underwater motion, pipeline tracking, and environmental sensing. A single robot can inspect a local region and collect useful measurements, while larger monitoring tasks require coordination across multiple agents. Cooperative inspection is useful when an infrastructure corridor, leak region, or environmental plume covers an area larger than one robot can sample efficiently during one mission. In that setting, each robot needs some estimate of where nearby robots are located so that the group can maintain spacing, avoid redundant sampling, and follow a shared inspection path.

Underwater localization is difficult because GPS is attenuated after submergence, visual sensing depends on lighting and turbidity, and acoustic localization hardware can become large, expensive, and power-demanding for compact robotic fish. The relative-localization work in this chapter therefore investigates a short-range sensing approach based on received signal strength (RSS) from sub-GHz RF links, directional antennas, and inertial/magnetic measurements \cite{Masood2025Swarm,Masood2026RSSI}. The goal is to estimate relative distance and bearing between an underwater leader node and a follower node, which is the information needed for leader-follower coordination.

This chapter combines two related developments. First, a leader-follower simulation framework is used to connect RSS-based relative localization with robotic-fish motion and formation keeping. Second, an experimental RSSI localization device is designed and evaluated in water-tank tests using four directional RF antenna channels, an IMU, and vision-based ground truth. The simulation establishes how the localization estimate would be used by robotic fish, while the experiment evaluates the sensing hardware and data-driven localization method.

\section{Relative Localization Model}

\subsection{Leader-Follower Configuration}

The relative-localization problem is written for a leader-follower pair. The leader represents a better-instrumented vehicle or reference node that can follow a pipeline, communicate with a surface system, or provide a reference signal for nearby followers. The follower carries a lower-cost localization unit and estimates its position relative to the leader using RSS measurements and inertial/magnetic sensing. This configuration is shown conceptually in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5_swarm_schematic}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{Fig/ch5_swarm_schematic.png}
    \caption{Leader-follower concept for RSS-based relative localization.}
    \label{fig:ch5_swarm_schematic}
\end{figure}

Let the leader position be $(x_\ell,y_\ell)$ and the follower position be $(x_f,y_f)$ in the world frame. The follower yaw angle is $\psi_f$. The relative range from follower to leader is
\begin{equation}
    d = \sqrt{(x_\ell-x_f)^2 + (y_\ell-y_f)^2}.
    \label{eq:ch5_relative_distance}
\end{equation}
The relative bearing in the follower body frame is
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y_\ell-y_f,x_\ell-x_f)-\psi_f.
    \label{eq:ch5_relative_bearing}
\end{equation}
The localization task is to estimate $d$ and $\theta$, and then reconstruct the relative Cartesian position of the follower with respect to the leader.

\subsection{Relative Motion Model}

For the leader-follower simulator, the follower state is written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_k & y_k & \psi_k & u_k & v_k & r_k & \dot{u}_k & \dot{v}_k
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}.
    \label{eq:ch5_sim_state}
\end{equation}
Here, $(x_k,y_k)$ and $\psi_k$ are the planar position and yaw angle, $u_k$, $v_k$, and $r_k$ are surge velocity, sway velocity, and yaw rate, and $\dot{u}_k$ and $\dot{v}_k$ are body-frame accelerations. With time step $\Delta t$, the propagated state used in the EKF simulation is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \hat{x}_k + \hat{u}_k \cos\hat{\psi}_k \Delta t - \hat{v}_k \sin\hat{\psi}_k \Delta t \\
    \hat{y}_k + \hat{u}_k \sin\hat{\psi}_k \Delta t + \hat{v}_k \cos\hat{\psi}_k \Delta t \\
    \hat{\psi}_k + \hat{r}_k \Delta t \\
    \hat{u}_k + \hat{\dot{u}}_k \Delta t \\
    \hat{v}_k + \hat{\dot{v}}_k \Delta t \\
    r_{m,k} \\
    \dot{u}_{m,k} \\
    \dot{v}_{m,k}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:ch5_motion_model}
\end{equation}
This state model provides the inertial propagation used in the simulation framework. In the experimental RSSI work, the inertial and magnetic measurements are used mainly for heading estimation and temporal correction of the RSSI-based bearing estimate.

\subsection{RSS-Based Measurement Model}

The RF unit uses multiple directional antennas to infer both range and direction from received signal strength. In the simulation and hardware design, four antennas are placed around the horizontal plane with known angular spacing. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5_swarm_rss_unit} shows the RSS unit geometry used in the leader-follower simulation.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.56\textwidth]{Fig/ch5_swarm_rss_unit.png}
    \caption{Directional RSS unit geometry in the simulator.}
    \label{fig:ch5_swarm_rss_unit}
\end{figure}

The received power at an antenna is modeled as
\begin{equation}
    P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r(\theta) - L(d),
    \label{eq:ch5_received_power}
\end{equation}
where $P_t$ is the transmitted power, $G_t$ is the transmitter gain, $G_r(\theta)$ is the directional receive gain, and $L(d)$ is the distance-dependent path loss. For the low-frequency RF model used in the papers, the attenuation is written as
\begin{equation}
    L(d)=\alpha_w d,
    \label{eq:ch5_path_loss}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_w = 0.0173\sqrt{f_{rf}\sigma},
    \label{eq:ch5_water_attenuation}
\end{equation}
where $f_{rf}$ is RF frequency and $\sigma$ is water conductivity. The receiving antenna pattern is approximated as
\begin{equation}
    G_r(\theta)=G_{\max}-12\left(\frac{\theta}{\alpha_b}\right)^2,
    \label{eq:ch5_directional_gain}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_b$ is the beam-width parameter. In the analytical RSS model, the strongest antenna and its adjacent antenna are used to estimate the local angle of arrival. If $S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the strongest RSS value and $S_{\mathrm{adj}}$ is the adjacent-channel RSS value, the RSS ratio is
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{ratio}} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{S_{\mathrm{adj}}}{S_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right).
    \label{eq:ch5_rss_ratio}
\end{equation}
The local relative angle used in the simulation model is
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    -\frac{1}{2}
    \left(
        90^\circ + \frac{\alpha_b^2}{90^\circ}\frac{S_{\mathrm{ratio}}}{12}
    \right),
    \label{eq:ch5_theta_rel}
\end{equation}
and the angle of arrival is
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\mathrm{AoA}}=\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}+\theta_{\mathrm{rel}}(\mathrm{ref}-\mathrm{adj}).
    \label{eq:ch5_aoa}
\end{equation}
The corresponding RSS-based range estimate is
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{RSS}} =
    \left\lVert
    \frac{(P_t)_{\mathrm{dB}} + (G(\theta_{\mathrm{rel}}))_{\mathrm{dB}} - (S_{\mathrm{ref}})_{\mathrm{dB}}}
    {0.0173\sqrt{f_{rf}\sigma}}
    \right\rVert.
    \label{eq:ch5_rss_distance}
\end{equation}

\subsection{IMU-Based State Propagation}

The IMU provides acceleration and yaw-rate measurements for short-term motion propagation. In the simulator, the measured accelerations and yaw rate are represented as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \ddot{x}_{m,k} &= \ddot{x}_k+\eta_{\mathrm{acc},k}+\nu_{\mathrm{acc},k},\\
    \ddot{y}_{m,k} &= \ddot{y}_k+\eta_{\mathrm{acc},k}+\nu_{\mathrm{acc},k},\\
    \dot{\psi}_{m,k} &= \dot{\psi}_k+\eta_{\mathrm{gyro},k}+\nu_{\mathrm{gyro},k},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ch5_imu_measurement}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ terms represent white noise and $\nu$ terms represent drift. The experimental device uses a 9-axis IMU, including accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer channels. The magnetometer gives an absolute heading cue, while the gyroscope provides short-horizon rotational information.

\subsection{Sensor Fusion Framework}

The simulation uses an EKF with two measurement updates: one update from the IMU and one update from the RSS-based relative-position estimate. The IMU measurement model is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{IMU},k} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \dot{u}_{m,k} & \dot{v}_{m,k} & r_{m,k}
    \end{bmatrix}^{T},
    \label{eq:ch5_imu_vector}
\end{equation}
and the RSS update supplies a position or relative-position correction derived from $d_{\mathrm{RSS}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{AoA}}$.

The experimental RSSI framework uses a physics-aware learning layer before fusion. The RSSI vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    S_{1,k} & S_{2,k} & S_{3,k} & S_{4,k}
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}
    \label{eq:ch5_rssi_vector}
\end{equation}
is combined with magnetometer and short-horizon gyroscope features to estimate distance, bearing, and yaw. The relative Cartesian position of the receiver with respect to the transmitter is then reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \hat{x}_{\mathrm{rel},k}\\
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{rel},k}
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    -\hat{d}_k
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos(\hat{\psi}_k+\hat{\theta}_k)\\
    \sin(\hat{\psi}_k+\hat{\theta}_k)
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:ch5_relative_position}
\end{equation}

\section[Simulation Framework for Leader-Follower Robotic Fish]{Simulation Framework for Leader-Follower\\ Robotic Fish}

\subsection{Simulator Architecture}

The simulation framework from the swarm paper is built around a water-tank environment containing a leader robotic fish, a follower robotic fish, a pipeline feature on the tank bottom, RF/RSS sensing, and IMU sensing \cite{Masood2025Swarm}. The environment maintains the robot states, updates the RF signal-strength field, computes simulated sensor outputs, and logs localization and formation-control variables. The simulator is used to test how RSS and IMU estimates support leader-follower formation before the physical RF localization device is evaluated experimentally.

\subsection{Robotic Fish Dynamic Model}

Both simulated robots are bio-inspired robotic fish using the planar robotic-fish model described in Chapter~2 and used for pipeline-tracking control in Chapter~3 \cite{Masood2025Pipeline}. The leader uses camera-based pipeline tracking to follow the line feature in the tank. The follower uses relative localization to estimate the leader position and adjust its tail actuation. This keeps the simulation connected to the AquaNavigator modeling work rather than treating the localization problem as a point-mass abstraction alone.

\subsection{Leader and Follower Trajectories}

The leader represents the better-instrumented robot. In the simulation, it carries the sensing and control capabilities needed to track the pipeline. The follower carries an RSS unit and IMU and estimates its relative position with respect to the leader. The formation-control objective is to maintain a desired inter-agent distance while following the leader path. In the reported simulation, the desired distance is
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{ref}} = 1~\mathrm{m}.
    \label{eq:ch5_dref}
\end{equation}
The follower control law regulates tail propulsion and steering using
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{tail}} =
\begin{cases}
k_p(d_{\mathrm{Fused}}-d_{\mathrm{ref}}), & d_{\mathrm{Fused}}>d_{\mathrm{ref}},\\
0, & d_{\mathrm{Fused}}\leq d_{\mathrm{ref}},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ch5_tail_speed_control}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\mathrm{tail}}=k_{\delta}\theta_{\mathrm{AoA}},
    \label{eq:ch5_tail_steering_control}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathrm{Fused}}$ is the fused relative-distance estimate and $\theta_{\mathrm{AoA}}$ is the estimated bearing.

\subsection{Simulated RSS and IMU Measurements}

The simulated RF field uses the attenuation model and directional antenna model defined earlier in this chapter. Each RSS unit has a transmitter and a receiver with four directional antennas. The receiver computes one RSS value per antenna channel, and the RSS pattern is converted into an angle-of-arrival and range estimate. The IMU model adds white noise and drift to acceleration and yaw-rate measurements. These two sensing streams are then fused in the EKF.

\subsection{Data Logging and Evaluation Metrics}

The simulator logs ground-truth leader and follower states, IMU-only estimates, RSS-only estimates, fused EKF estimates, relative distance, bearing, and formation errors. The main simulation metrics are the localization error between the estimated and ground-truth follower positions and the formation errors
\begin{equation}
    e_d = d_{\mathrm{RSS}} - \sqrt{(x_f-x_\ell)^2+(y_f-y_\ell)^2},
    \label{eq:ch5_sim_distance_error}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    e_{\theta} = \theta_{\mathrm{AoA}}-(\psi_f-\psi_\ell).
    \label{eq:ch5_sim_bearing_error}
\end{equation}
These quantities are used in Sec.~\ref{sec:ch5_results_discussion} to compare IMU-only, RSS-only, and fused localization behavior.

\section{Design of the RSS Localization Device}

\subsection{Sub-GHz RF Localization Concept}

The experimental localization device uses sub-GHz RF because lower RF frequencies attenuate less severely in freshwater than common 2.4~GHz radio links. The device uses a $433.92$~MHz carrier and measures RSSI from multiple antenna channels. At this frequency, the wavelength is approximately $0.69$~m, so phase-based direction-of-arrival would require antenna spacing that is large relative to a small robotic fish. The device therefore uses directional antenna response and RSSI differences as the practical basis for bearing estimation.

\subsection{Directional Antenna Arrangement}

The localization device uses four flat antenna elements placed at $90^\circ$ angular spacing around the body frame. The antenna design is shaped to create directional sensitivity in the horizontal plane. The paper reports a compact antenna modification using RF absorber material and an asymmetric ground-plane approach to obtain at least a 10~dB front-to-back difference in the azimuth plane \cite{Masood2026RSSI}. This directional behavior is central to the RSSI bearing estimate because the received signal pattern across channels changes with arrival direction.

\subsection{Mechanical Base and Sensor Layout}

The mechanical device is designed as a compact waterproof localization module. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5_rssi_device_description} shows the four antenna channels and the internal sensor layout. The reported package size is approximately $15~\mathrm{cm}\times15~\mathrm{cm}\times8~\mathrm{cm}$. The enclosure is fabricated as a watertight body with flange sealing and penetrators for wired connections. This form factor is intended for small underwater platforms where localization hardware must be replicated across several agents.

The experimental validation is performed in a controlled water-tank environment. Before localization data collection, the RF link is tested independently in the tank to verify packet reception. The receiver recorded signal strength at a distance of 10~m when the transmitter and receiver were held at a depth of 0.5~m. For localization data collection, the device is mounted on a BlueROV, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch5_rssi_hardware_mounted}, while a transmitter is placed at a fixed location.

\subsection{Ground-Truth Measurement System}

An AprilTag is attached to the moving platform, and an overhead camera provides ground-truth position and orientation. This produces the reference quantities needed to train and evaluate the learned localization models. The ground-truth data are synchronized with the RSSI and IMU recordings through host-side logging.

\subsection{RSS and IMU Data Collection}

Each sample contains the RSSI vector from four antennas, accelerometer data, gyroscope data, magnetometer data, ground-truth position, ground-truth yaw, and timestamps from the device and computer. Two main datasets are reported: one covering 0.5--3~m range and a second covering 0.5--6~m range. The antenna depth is controlled during collection because the water surface and tank boundaries affect multipath and RSSI patterns.

\subsection{Dataset Construction and Preprocessing}

The dataset is constructed by aligning each RSSI and IMU sample with its corresponding ground-truth pose. Angular targets are represented with sine and cosine components to avoid discontinuity at the wrap-around boundary. Magnetometer features include the planar components, magnitude, and direct heading representation. RSSI features include raw or normalized antenna-channel values. The resulting feature sets are used to train separate estimators for yaw, distance, and bearing.

\section{Learning-Based Localization Method}

\subsection{Feature Representation}

The learning method is physics-aware in the sense that it keeps the geometry and periodic structure of the localization problem in the feature representation. For yaw estimation, the planar magnetometer feature vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}^{(m)}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    m_{x,k} & m_{y,k} & r_k & \cos\varphi_k & \sin\varphi_k
    \end{bmatrix}^{T},
    \label{eq:ch5_mag_features}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    r_k=\sqrt{m_{x,k}^2+m_{y,k}^2}, \qquad
    \varphi_k=\operatorname{atan2}(m_{y,k},m_{x,k}).
    \label{eq:ch5_mag_radius_heading}
\end{equation}
For distance estimation, the feature vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}^{(d)}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{S}_k^{T} & m_{x,k} & m_{y,k} & \|\mathbf{m}_{xy,k}\|_2 & \cos\varphi_k & \sin\varphi_k
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}.
    \label{eq:ch5_distance_features}
\end{equation}
For angular predictions, the target is represented as $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and recovered by $\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)$.

\subsection{RF-Only Localization}

The RF-only localization case uses RSSI measurements to estimate distance and bearing. In the experimental data, the relation between RSSI and distance is affected by directional antenna gain, tank reflections, radiation-lobe behavior, and receiver orientation. For this reason, the experimental method learns the measurement-to-state relationship from data rather than relying only on the analytical path-loss inversion. The RF-only case is still important because it shows how much localization information is contained in the antenna-channel RSSI pattern itself.

\subsection{RF-IMU Fusion}

The RF-IMU method uses magnetometer and gyroscope information to improve heading and bearing consistency. The short-horizon gyroscope feature is built from the bias-corrected yaw-rate increment
\begin{equation}
    \delta\psi_k=(\omega_k-b_g)\Delta t_k,
    \label{eq:ch5_delta_psi}
\end{equation}
and the accumulated short-horizon increment
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\psi_k^{(L)}
    =
    \sum_{i=\max(0,k-L+1)}^{k}\delta\psi_i.
    \label{eq:ch5_short_horizon_gyro}
\end{equation}
This gives the gyroscope feature vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}^{(g)}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos(\Delta\psi_k^{(L)}) &
    \sin(\Delta\psi_k^{(L)}) &
    \Delta\psi_k^{(L)}
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}.
    \label{eq:ch5_gyro_features}
\end{equation}
For bearing correction, the raw RSSI-based bearing estimate $\hat{\theta}_k^{(\mathrm{RSSI})}$ is filtered using a yaw-guided Kalman correction. The prediction step is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta}_k^{-}
    =
    \hat{\theta}_{k-1}^{+}
    -
    \Delta\hat{\psi}_k,
    \label{eq:ch5_theta_prediction}
\end{equation}
and the measurement is
\begin{equation}
    z_k=\hat{\theta}_k^{(\mathrm{RSSI})}.
    \label{eq:ch5_theta_measurement}
\end{equation}
The update uses the wrapped angular innovation
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{y}_k =
    \operatorname{wrap}(z_k-\hat{\theta}_k^{-}).
    \label{eq:ch5_wrapped_innovation}
\end{equation}

Multiple regression methods are trained and compared using the same feature representation. The tested models include Extra Trees, Lasso regression, K-nearest neighbors, decision tree, neural network, gradient boosting, support vector regression, random forest, and ridge regression. The models are evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination $R^2$. The RF-only distance result shows the difficulty of using RSSI magnitude alone. This difference shows that receiver orientation and antenna directivity are strongly coupled with RSSI magnitude in the tank experiment. The RF-only channel still provides useful distance information, but the learned model performs better when heading-related features are included.

\subsection{Discussion}

The simulation and experiment address different parts of the same thesis problem. The simulation shows how a follower robotic fish can use relative distance and bearing estimates to maintain formation with a leader during pipeline-oriented motion. The experiment shows how compact sub-GHz RF hardware, directional antenna channels, and IMU/magnetometer sensing can estimate relative position in a controlled water-tank setting. Together, they provide a path from a single AquaNavigator-style platform toward cooperative underwater monitoring.

The experimental results also show why the localization method needs a data-driven component. RSSI depends on distance, arrival direction, antenna response, enclosure effects, water-tank reflections, and sensor orientation. The analytical model explains the structure of the problem, while the learning model captures the nonlinear mapping observed in the physical data. The strongest result is the reported $0.24$~m mean absolute position error over 3~m and $0.64$~m over 6~m. The remaining challenge is robustness across different tanks, depths, water conductivities, antenna mounting conditions, and multi-agent geometries.

\section{Future Work}

Future work should first integrate the experimental RSSI localization device with AquaNavigator so that the relative-localization estimator can be evaluated on the same robotic-fish platform used for pipeline tracking and environmental sensing. The next step is onboard implementation of the learning and fusion pipeline, including real-time packet handling, feature computation, and relative-position estimation on embedded hardware. The current experimental workflow uses host-side processing, while a cooperative robotic-fish system needs onboard estimation and communication.

The second direction is multi-agent validation. The present experimental evaluation uses one transmitter and one moving receiver, while cooperative monitoring requires multiple followers, changing relative geometry, packet scheduling, and collision-safe coordination. Additional work is also needed to test different water conductivities, depths, antenna orientations, enclosure materials, and environmental disturbances. These studies would make the learned RF model more transferable across operating conditions.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter presented an RSS-based relative-localization framework for underwater robotic swarms. The leader-follower simulation showed how RSS and IMU measurements can be fused for relative localization and used for formation control of robotic fish. The experimental work developed a compact $433.92$~MHz RSSI localization device with four directional antenna channels and a 9-axis IMU. The learning-based localization method used RSSI, magnetometer, and gyroscope-derived features to estimate relative distance, bearing, yaw, and position. The reported results achieved mean absolute localization errors of $0.24$~m over a 3~m range and $0.64$~m over a 6~m range. This chapter extends the dissertation from single-robot inspection and environmental sensing toward cooperative underwater monitoring.

\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}

This dissertation developed a bio-inspired robotic fish system for underwater inspection and environmental monitoring. The work began from the need for compact underwater robots that can operate near infrastructure, carry sensing payloads, regulate depth, and provide a path toward cooperative monitoring. The main thesis argument is that a robotic fish should be treated as an integrated system: the mechanical platform, buoyancy device, dynamic model, control algorithms, sensing payloads, and localization framework must work together for underwater monitoring tasks.

The first part of the dissertation developed the physical platform. AquaNavigator was designed as a tail-actuated robotic fish with a double-slider-crank caudal-fin mechanism, a steerable tail module, a watertight electronics enclosure, onboard camera feedback, embedded control electronics, and space for payload integration. A piston-cylinder buoyancy control device was also developed to support vertical motion by changing displaced volume. The platform design established the hardware basis for the later control, inspection, environmental-sensing, and relative-localization studies.

The second part of the dissertation modeled and controlled the buoyancy and swimming behavior. The BCD model coupled piston motion, hydrostatic loading, pressure effects, actuator dynamics, and vertical motion. The open-loop validation showed an actuator-position RMSE of $1.96$~mm and a depth-response RMSE of $23.07$~mm. These results showed that the actuator dynamics were captured more tightly than the full depth response, where drag variation, tank-wall effects, and neutral-buoyancy offsets remained important. The MPC controller used the model to regulate depth while respecting piston constraints, and the experimental MPC result reported steady-state tracking within approximately two percent error bounds. The ESC-tuned PD controller then addressed model dependence by adapting controller gains from measured performance while respecting actuator voltage and stroke limits.

The planar robotic-fish model and visual-servoing work connected the platform to infrastructure inspection. The pipeline-tracking controller used image-space pipeline features to command the tail-bias angle. In pool experiments, the robotic fish reduced the image-space heading error and converged to within $\psi_e=\pm 5^\circ$ of the pipeline across the listed tests. This result established pipeline following as a first inspection behavior for the platform and provided the motion behavior later used in the oil-sensing experiment.

The third part of the dissertation extended the platform from motion control to environmental sensing. In the \mbox{CO$_2$} leak-localization study, AquaNavigator carried a near-infrared \mbox{CO$_2$} sensor and collected position-tagged concentration measurements in a controlled tank. The data were processed through heatmaps, interpolation, peak detection, and cumulative time-window analysis. The source-estimation error decreased from 2.43~m after 75~s to 1.50~m after 151~s and reached 0.69~m after 527~s. This demonstrated that the robotic fish can collect spatially distributed chemical measurements and support data-driven source estimation in a controlled underwater setting.

The oil-spill sensing study integrated an OECT sensor with AquaNavigator. The work included compact signal conditioning, waterproof packaging, sensor placement on the robotic fish, laboratory characterization, and robotic pool testing during pipeline following. The OECT response separated clean-water, direct-oil, oil-contaminated-water, and robotic pool conditions. In the integrated robotic test, an oil leak introduced at 5~ml/min produced a delayed sensor response of approximately 60~s, consistent with oil absorption time in the OECT channel. Longer exposure at 15~min and 30~min produced stronger response reduction. This demonstrated the feasibility of using AquaNavigator as a mobile carrier for chemical oil detection during an inspection-like task.

The final technical part of the dissertation addressed relative localization for cooperative inspection. A leader-follower simulation showed how RSS and IMU measurements can be fused for formation control of robotic fish. The experimental localization device used a $433.92$~MHz RF link, four directional antenna channels, and a 9-axis IMU. The hardware test reported packet reception at 10~m distance and 0.5~m depth, and the learned RF-IMU localization framework achieved mean absolute position errors of 0.24~m over a 3~m range and 0.64~m over a 6~m range. This work connected the single-robot monitoring platform to the longer-term problem of cooperative robotic-fish inspection.

Taken together, the dissertation shows a progression from platform development to controlled motion, from controlled motion to sensing, and from single-robot sensing to relative localization for cooperation. The main contribution is the integrated development of a robotic fish platform and supporting methods for underwater monitoring: a compact bio-inspired vehicle, a variable buoyancy system for depth regulation, model-based and adaptive control methods, camera-guided pipeline tracking, chemical sensing payloads for \mbox{CO$_2$} and oil monitoring, and an RSS-based relative-localization framework for cooperative operation.

\section{Main Findings}

Several system-level findings emerge from the work. First, compact robotic fish design requires co-design of mechanics, waterproofing, sensing, and control. The tail mechanism, enclosure, camera placement, buoyancy hardware, and payload integration all affect the behavior of the same vehicle. Treating each subsystem independently would miss important interactions such as tail-induced image oscillation, payload placement effects, actuator limits, and waterproofing constraints.

Second, depth regulation with a piston-based BCD is a constrained control problem. The buoyancy mechanism provides a compact route to vertical motion, but its useful behavior depends on actuator stroke, voltage bounds, pressure coupling, friction, and neutral-buoyancy balance. The BCD results show the value of combining physical modeling, open-loop validation, model-based constrained control, and adaptive tuning.

Third, inspection and environmental sensing require more than robot locomotion. Pipeline tracking, \mbox{CO$_2$} source localization, and oil-spill detection all require the robot to connect sensor measurements to motion. In this dissertation, the camera provides image-space guidance for pipeline following, the \mbox{CO$_2$} sensor provides spatial concentration measurements for source estimation, and the OECT sensor provides chemical response during robotic motion. The common role of AquaNavigator is to carry the sensing payload through a controlled underwater environment and generate interpretable measurements.

Fourth, cooperative monitoring requires relative localization methods that are compatible with small underwater platforms. The RSSI work shows that sub-GHz RF measurements contain useful short-range localization information in controlled freshwater conditions, especially when directional antennas and IMU/magnetometer features are used together. The same results also show that antenna directivity, multipath, enclosure effects, and water-tank geometry shape the measurement model, which motivates data-driven compensation.

\section{Future Work}

The next stage of this research is to move from component-level demonstrations toward an integrated autonomous monitoring system. The work presented in this dissertation developed the main building blocks of such a system: a bio-inspired robotic fish platform, a buoyancy-based depth-control mechanism, pipeline-tracking control, environmental sensing payloads, and an RSS-based relative-localization framework. Future work should focus on combining these capabilities into a coordinated three-dimensional robotic platform that can navigate near underwater infrastructure, regulate its depth, collect environmental measurements, and communicate or localize relative to nearby agents.

A major direction is full three-dimensional experimental integration of AquaNavigator. The current demonstrations evaluate horizontal motion, vertical buoyancy control, sensing, and localization in separate or partially connected settings. A future integrated experiment should combine tail-driven propulsion, BCD-based depth regulation, visual pipeline tracking, and environmental sensing in one closed-loop mission. This would allow the robot to regulate its position relative to an underwater structure while simultaneously collecting chemical or visual measurements. Such experiments would also reveal coupling effects that are not fully captured when subsystems are tested independently, such as the influence of body motion on sensor response, the effect of depth changes on perception, and the interaction between buoyancy control and forward swimming.

The hardware platform should also be improved for longer and more reliable operation. Important directions include better neutral-buoyancy calibration, reduced actuator backlash, improved sealing, more robust cable and connector management, and modular payload interfaces for different sensors. The buoyancy-control device should be tested under a wider range of depths and disturbance conditions, while the controller implementation should include stronger sensor-noise filtering and more systematic model identification. These improvements would make the platform more repeatable and would reduce the gap between controlled laboratory demonstrations and field-relevant operation.

The perception and sensing capabilities should be extended to more realistic underwater conditions. Pipeline tracking should be tested under varying lighting, turbidity, partial occlusion, curved paths, and cluttered backgrounds. The image-processing and control pipeline should also be moved toward embedded or real-time implementation to reduce dependence on a host computer. For environmental sensing, future work should account for sensor response time, water flow, diffusion, advection, and motion-induced measurement delay. In the \mbox{CO$_2$} case, different leak rates, flow conditions, and robot trajectories should be tested. In the oil-spill case, the OECT sensing module should be evaluated for repeatability, recovery behavior, concentration sensitivity, fouling, debris, and response-time compensation during robot motion.

The relative-localization framework should be developed into a practical cooperative-monitoring capability. The RSS localization device should be miniaturized and integrated with AquaNavigator or a similar compact underwater robot. Future datasets should include different water conductivities, antenna depths, antenna placements, enclosure materials, robot orientations, and tank or pool geometries. A more systematic characterization of underwater antenna directivity is also needed, since the localization performance depends strongly on installation geometry and environmental conditions. The learning and fusion framework should be evaluated in real time, with uncertainty estimation and robustness testing across independently collected datasets.

At the mission level, future work should connect autonomy, sensing, and cooperation. A practical monitoring system would require mission planning, onboard state estimation, power management, health monitoring, fault handling, and data logging. Multiple robotic fish could then be deployed in leader-follower or cooperative formations to monitor larger regions around subsea pipelines or storage sites. In such a system, one robot may follow an infrastructure feature while others maintain relative position, collect complementary environmental measurements, and adapt their paths based on detected concentration gradients or contamination cues. This would extend the present work from a set of validated robotic subsystems toward a scalable bio-inspired platform for autonomous subsea infrastructure and environmental monitoring.

\section{Closing Remarks}

This dissertation contributes a system-level step toward compact robotic fish for underwater monitoring. The work developed the AquaNavigator platform, modeled and controlled its buoyancy and swimming behavior, demonstrated pipeline tracking and chemical sensing tasks, and introduced RSS-based relative localization for cooperative inspection. The results are grounded in laboratory and pool experiments, supported by simulation where appropriate, and organized around a consistent thesis direction: bio-inspired robotic fish can serve as mobile sensing platforms for underwater infrastructure and environmental monitoring, with future extension toward coordinated multi-robot operation.
